## Publisher’s Foreword

The struggle between truth and falsehood began when Iblis defied God’s command and refused to bow before Adam. Out of envy, hatred, and pride, he vowed to lead Adam and his descendants astray and turn them away from the straight path.

From that moment, two camps took shape. On one side stood the camp of truth: God’s prophets, messengers, and righteous servants. On the other stood the camp of falsehood: Iblis and his followers among both human beings and jinn.

That struggle has never ended. It reaches into every part of human life. Each person faces trials of their own, both as an individual and as a member of society. And for the believer, the first responsibility in this struggle is the struggle within oneself.

Karbala witnessed one of the fiercest clashes between these two camps.

There, a woman who had been terribly wronged stood among the bodies of loved ones who had been killed unjustly. She endured captivity and humiliation, then found herself standing before the tyrants responsible for what had happened. They asked her a question meant to break her—but the question exposed them instead and revealed the true nature of the conflict:

> “How did you see what God did to your brother?”

Bound in chains, she answered:

> “I saw nothing but beauty.”

That is patience at its most beautiful.

It is the kind of patience Sayyid Hassan Nasrallah, Secretary-General of Hezbollah, spoke about throughout the nights of Ashura in 1438 AH at the Sayyid al-Shuhada Complex.

Because of the importance of these lectures, we felt they should be edited, collected in a book, and made available to readers.

We ask Almighty God to grant us, and you, a good end and the greatest victory.

# Chapter One

## The True Meaning of Husayn’s Victory: In This Life and the Next

## Introduction

Before getting into the details, I want to spend a little time on the theme we chose for Ashura this year: **“Patience and Victory.”**[^part01-p014-n1]

There are several ideas contained in those two words, and they will help guide the subjects we want to discuss.

For us, Ashura is a story of patience and victory: patience in the face of suffering, leading ultimately to victory.

But this immediately raises a question.

Some people may find it strange that we describe Ashura as a victory at all. After all, this was a battle in which the leader was martyred, his fighters were martyred, the women were taken captive, and all the terrible events we know from Karbala took place.

So how can we look at what happened—and at an outcome like that—and call it a **victory**?

I want to answer this question as objectively as possible. I will try to avoid emotional rhetoric and stirring slogans. Instead, I will rely on reasoned arguments grounded in what actually happened at Ashura.

And I do not want to treat the subject as history alone. We will also look at our own time through the experience of the past and ask what lessons we can draw from it.

## What Does Victory Actually Mean?

Whenever two sides go to war—two fronts, two blocs, or simply two groups—some people will be martyred and others wounded. Some may be taken prisoner. There may be widespread destruction, followed by all the grief and pain that comes with it.

These are things no military conflict can completely avoid. Both sides can suffer them. No matter how many precautions a side takes, it cannot protect itself from every loss.

So losses of this kind do not, by themselves, tell us who won.

What does?

Real victory has to be measured by a different kind of result.

I do not want to bury the subject in complicated technical language, so let us use examples from wars and conflicts we ourselves have lived through in recent years.

Broadly speaking, there are **two levels of victory**. They are different, but in some cases they can come together.

The first level is this:

**You prevent your enemy from achieving their goals.**

Any enemy that begins a war or launches an attack does so with certain objectives in mind. It commits its military strength in the hope of achieving goals that it believes will advance its strategic interests.

At this first level, it is enough to prevent the enemy from achieving all of its goals—or at the very least, its main goals—even if it still manages to achieve a few secondary or limited ones.

That is enough to call the outcome a victory.

Take the July 2006 war.

The Zionist enemy entered its war against Lebanon with a set of objectives. Some were military, some political, some concerned the situation on the ground, and others reached beyond Lebanon to the wider region and what was then being called the “Greater Middle East.” Alongside these major goals were a number of smaller ones.

When the resistance prevented the enemy from achieving the main objectives of its attack, the resistance had won.

That remains true even though people were martyred and enormous destruction took place. Those are among the natural consequences of war. They do not, by themselves, determine who won.

There is also a **second and higher level of victory**.

At this level, it is not enough simply to stop your enemy from achieving their goals. You also achieve your own.

Consider the Israeli invasion of Lebanon in 1982.

That invasion had military, security, and political objectives involving Lebanon, the Palestinian cause, the Palestinian resistance, Syria, and the region as a whole. One of its most important aims was to bring Lebanon under Israeli control.

The resistance, in turn, set out to stop the enemy from achieving those objectives. But it also developed goals of its own: to mobilize the people, wear down the enemy, and ultimately liberate the land and the prisoners without conditions, without favors, and without giving the enemy anything in return.

That struggle continued from 1985 until 2000.

Then came 25 May 2000.

On that day, the whole world saw that the resistance had done both things.

First, it had prevented the Israeli enemy from achieving any of the objectives of its 1982 invasion.

At the same time, it had achieved its own: it had worn down the enemy, mobilized the people, set an example, liberated the land and the prisoners, and restored sovereignty without conditions and without giving anything in return.

That is the highest level of victory.

## Ashura and the Confrontation Between Husayn and Yazid

Now let us return to the historical events of Ashura.

History presents us with two camps.

On one side stood Imam Husayn and the small group who remained with him.

On the other stood Yazid and his enormous army.

Each side entered the confrontation with goals of its own.

But if we want to decide who truly won and who truly lost, we have to look at those goals in **two different ways**: in terms of this world, and in terms of the life to come.

That distinction matters.

A side may achieve victory in the next life by seeking God’s approval, struggling in His cause, and giving everything it has for Him, while at the same time suffering defeat in worldly terms because it failed to achieve any of its immediate goals.

The opposite is also possible. A side may achieve success in this world while, by the standards of the next life, ending in complete failure and defeat.

So when we speak of Husayn’s victory—the victory we celebrate, carry as a banner, and take as an example in all our struggles—what kind of victory do we mean?

Was it victory by one of these measures but not the other?

Or was it victory by both?

We will now look at each in turn and try to reach a clear answer.

## The Goals of the Conflict in This World

Let us begin with the worldly side of the question.

What did Yazid want?

When Muawiya ibn Abi Sufyan died, power and the caliphate passed to his son Yazid.

Yazid wanted the entire Muslim community to pledge allegiance to him, by force if necessary. But several leading figures in the Muslim world refused. Foremost among them was Imam Husayn.

Yazid therefore wrote to the governor of Medina and ordered him to obtain pledges of allegiance from Husayn ibn Ali, Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr, Abdullah ibn Umar, and others. He followed this with another order: anyone who refused was to be executed.

Through this decision, and through the events that followed from it, Yazid was trying to achieve a number of things.

One of them is not seriously disputed by either Sunni or Shia Muslims: **he wanted to secure his rule and consolidate his power.**

He wanted to remain in power for as long as possible and establish firm control.

But his authority over the Muslim community could never be completely secure while major figures among the Prophet’s Companions and family refused to recognize him. He therefore needed their allegiance. Without it, his rule would remain unstable.

Husayn’s allegiance mattered especially.

Imam Husayn was one of the surviving members of the Prophet’s household and of the People of the Cloak. He was the grandson of God’s Prophet—and there was no other grandson of a prophet living on earth.

For that reason, Yazid was particularly determined to obtain Husayn’s pledge of allegiance and the legitimacy that would come with it.

Another of Yazid’s unquestionable aims was to establish a stable Sufyani branch of Umayyad rule: a system that would keep political power and kingship within the family of Abu Sufyan after his own death, passing it on to Abu Sufyan’s descendants.

If we go further and look at Yazid’s deeper intentions—and this is a view accepted within Shia thought and by a significant part of the Sunni tradition as well—then the argument is that his ambitions went beyond what I have just described.

His character, his thinking, his intentions, and his actions pointed to something larger.

His conduct threatened Islam itself.

The aim, according to this reading, was to remove the religion of Muhammad ibn Abdullah from the culture, consciousness, and moral life of the Muslim community, and to take the community back to a new age of pre-Islamic ignorance under an oppressive hereditary monarchy ruled by the house of Abu Sufyan.

The events recorded by history are offered as evidence for this.

First came Yazid’s treatment of Imam Husayn and the Prophet’s family.

Then came what he did in the city of the Prophet to the Prophet’s Companions among the Emigrants and the Helpers, and to their sons and daughters.

After that came his decision to besiege Mecca and order it bombarded with catapults until it surrendered, while Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr was holding out there.

Taken together, these actions and others like them support the argument that Yazid’s goal was not merely to seize and hold political power. His project struck at Islam itself.

There was also another motive, one that could be described as secondary or nearly central in its own right:

**revenge.**

Revenge against the Messenger of God, against his family, and against his Companions.

This was one of the clear aims Yazid pursued during his rule, and his pursuit of it can be seen in Karbala, then in Medina, and later in Mecca.

In the same way, if we turn to Husayn’s own aims, we can say in broad terms that his main goal in confronting Yazid was to stop Yazid from achieving his goals. At the same time, he was working constantly to protect Islam, strengthen it, and ensure its survival.

If we follow the same sequence and look at Husayn’s aims in more detail, the first was **to deny Yazid ibn Muawiya legitimacy**. Yazid was, in Husayn’s view, a tyrant who threatened both Islam and the dignity of the Muslim community. Husayn therefore refused absolutely to pledge allegiance to him, hoping to shake his rule and bring it down.

This was clear throughout his journey: from Medina to Mecca, from Mecca to Karbala, through siege and thirst, right up to the final moments of his life. Husayn had set himself this goal, and he never abandoned it.

Another of his goals was **to expose Yazid for what he really was**.

The lack of adequate communication at the time had led many people to believe that Yazid was a man of faith and learning, a devout worshipper and an ascetic. Many had pledged allegiance to him on precisely that understanding.

Husayn therefore wanted Muslims to see the truth about this ruler and the false image surrounding him. Exposing Yazid was essential if his rule and his political project were to be prevented from taking firm hold.

Another aim was **to awaken the Muslim community**.

For twenty years, people had lived under the rule of Muawiya ibn Abi Sufyan. Over time, they had grown used to submission and passivity. They had lost much of their determination, will, and enthusiasm, and many facts had been distorted or concealed from them.

They had to be shaken awake.

Words alone were no longer enough. A bloody confrontation, followed by the terrible injustice of Husayn’s martyrdom, could awaken them in a way that speeches could not.

And above all these aims stood the greatest one: **to preserve Islam, ensure its survival, and defend it**. In Husayn’s view, this required the fall of Yazid ibn Muawiya, a ruler whose project threatened Islam itself.

These, then, were Yazid’s goals, and these were Husayn’s.

Now that we have identified them, we can look at what actually happened and then judge the outcome in worldly terms.

## The Confrontation and What Followed

Yazid demanded Husayn’s allegiance. Husayn refused.

He refused first in Medina, then in Mecca, and continued to refuse as he made his way toward Iraq and finally Karbala.

There the bloody confrontation took place. Husayn and those with him were martyred, while the women of the Prophet’s household were taken captive. They were carried from Kufa through other Iraqi cities, then through cities in Syria, until they reached Damascus, before eventually returning to Medina.

News of Husayn’s martyrdom spread throughout the Muslim world, and the whole community was shaken.

Husayn was not some obscure or ordinary figure. Everyone knew who he was and understood his standing. His killing therefore sent shock waves through the Muslim community. People were horrified that the ruling caliph had killed the grandson of the Messenger of God.

This unrest soon gave rise to movements that would later become a series of revolts.

The scandal also reached Syria. A delegation from Medina travelled there, and through that visit people came to see Yazid’s true character.

The visit had another major effect.

The members of that delegation had apparently been confused about Yazid, like many people in Medina. But when they travelled to Syria and met him themselves, they discovered that Husayn’s description of him was accurate. They found him to be, in their view, a corrupt and immoral man who killed innocent people and possessed the other vices Husayn had warned about.

They now understood why Husayn had so firmly refused to pledge allegiance to him.

And beyond all these vices, they could see none of the virtues expected of a Muslim ruler.

They returned to Medina and told the people what they had seen. Medina then withdrew its allegiance from Yazid.

Yazid responded by sending an army against the city.

This was Medina, the city of the Messenger of God: the city containing his grave, the graves of his Companions, and so many other reminders of the blessed age of the Prophet.

This happened in Dhu al-Hijjah of the year 63 AH. Some of the Prophet’s surviving Companions from among the Emigrants and the Helpers were still living there with their families, as were surviving members of the Prophet’s household.

Yazid placed the army under the command of a man named Muslim ibn Uqba. Sunni and Shia historical works often refer to him instead as **Musrif ibn Uqba**, and curse his memory.

He laid siege to Medina. The city refused to surrender and fought back, but it soon fell.

Musrif then entered Medina under Yazid’s orders. According to the historical accounts, Yazid had given him permission to hand the city over to his soldiers for three days.

And that is what he did.

Accounts of the event say that Yazid’s soldiers entered the Prophet’s Mosque with their mules and animals and violated the sanctity of the Prophet’s grave. People were killed and blood was shed throughout the city of the Messenger of God.

Historical works also state that within a year, or less than a year, of the attack, a thousand Muslim women in Medina gave birth despite having no husbands—an indication, according to these accounts, that they had been raped.

Let me also give you a set of figures found in the historical works. Some of these books attribute them to a leading Umayyad scholar from the later Marwanid period.

According to this account, seven hundred prominent members of the Emigrants and Helpers were killed during those three days, along with ten thousand ordinary people.

Only a small number of them, it says, died in the fighting itself. The rest were brought forward, killed, and beheaded. Bodies piled up and blood flowed through the city.

Had that scene been filmed and shown to humanity generation after generation, no Muslim—or indeed any human being—could have dared defend Yazid ibn Muawiya or remained unaware of what Yazid and his project really represented.

I want to pause here and look at the Muslim world today, because there is a comparison to be made between Yazid and his followers and some contemporary extremist groups that declare other Muslims unbelievers.

One respected scholar made a point that caught my attention, and I agree with him completely. He said:

**ISIS is not a new version of the Kharijites. It is a new version of the Umayyads.**

If we want to understand movements such as ISIS and others like it, then we have to look for their roots and foundations in Muslim history.

Look at what happened in Medina, the city of the Prophet, in the year 63 AH.

And you do not have to consult Shia books to do so. Read the works of our Sunni brothers and see what Sunni scholars themselves have written about Yazid and Musrif ibn Uqba.

Returning to Musrif: after committing those atrocities, he demanded a new pledge of allegiance from the people who remained in Medina.

But allegiance on what terms?

Did this blood-soaked killer merely ask them to recognize Yazid ibn Muawiya as caliph, ruler, imam, and political leader?

No.

He demanded that they pledge that they themselves, their children, and their property belonged to Yazid ibn Muawiya, and that they were his slaves.

Anyone who refused was killed and beheaded.

Worse still, once he had finished with Medina, Musrif intended—again under orders from his master Yazid—to march on Mecca, besiege it, and sack it just as Medina had been sacked.

The people of Mecca were to be forced to give the same pledge: that they too were Yazid’s slaves and property.

But before Musrif could carry out this new mission, he fell ill and was unable to continue. He handed command to someone even worse than himself.

One striking detail comes from Musrif’s final testament. He is reported to have said:

> “God, after my testimony that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is the Messenger of God, I have never done any good deed dearer to me than killing the people of Medina, nor any deed in which I place greater hope for the next life.”

These men believed their actions and crimes brought them closer to God.

And today we see the same thing with ISIS and groups like it. They believe they draw closer to God through slaughter, captivity, killing, violation of sacred things, and the destruction of holy places.

They are the heirs of Musrif ibn Uqba, carrying his way of thinking and his project.

The new commander marched to Mecca and placed it under siege. History records that Yazid’s army bombarded Mecca with catapults.

But the siege did not last long.

Yazid ibn Muawiya died in Rabi al-Awwal of the year 64 AH.

To this day, there is no firmly established account of how he died. Some say he was killed. Others say he died in an accident. None of these accounts has been conclusively proven, and no body of Yazid survives that could settle the matter.

After his death, power passed to his designated heir, his son Muawiya ibn Yazid.

Only a few days later, Muawiya renounced the caliphate. He died a few weeks after that, and his death too remains mysterious.

With him, the Sufyani branch of the ruling house collapsed in Rabi al-Thani of 64 AH—less than four years after the Battle of Karbala.

Those, then, were the events of the confrontation and its immediate aftermath.

Now we can turn to the results, which should be much clearer after everything we have just described.

## The Results of the Confrontation

If we judge the confrontation between Husayn and Yazid by the worldly standards we have been discussing, the first thing we notice is that **Islam survived**.

Yazid, in this account, wanted to destroy its foundations: the Prophet’s household, his Companions, the Emigrants and the Helpers, from Medina all the way to Mecca.

Yet Islam survived.

It spread, grew, and expanded, both in numbers and in influence, until today the Muslim community numbers more than 1.4 billion people.

Whatever their school, denomination, or sect, the survival of their religion is owed to Husayn’s blood and his sacrifice at Karbala.

Without that sacrifice, none of the Islamic schools that exist today would even have had the chance to survive.

Remember that Muslims at that time had not yet divided into Sunni and Shia schools as they later would. The religion of all of them was under threat, and it was Husayn’s blood that preserved the spirit and reality of Islam.

So the first result of the confrontation was the survival and preservation of Islam.

This is why some scholars interpret the second half of the Prophet’s saying:

> “Husayn is from me, and I am from Husayn.”

They take this to mean that Islam survived, endured, and preserved its teachings through Husayn.

The second result was the survival of the name and memory of Muhammad.

One of Yazid’s aims, according to this reading, was to erase the memory of Muhammad and his family.

But what happened was precisely the opposite.

Their names are remembered across the world to this day, while almost nothing remains of Yazid except his reputation.

This is why Lady Zaynab, when she spoke before Yazid in his own court, told him that the goals he had set for this confrontation had already failed.

Speaking of the survival of Islam, she told him:

> “You will never extinguish our revelation.”

And speaking of the Prophet’s household:

> “You will never erase our memory.”

Third, **Yazid’s rule lost its legitimacy**.

The killing of Husayn and the captivity of the women of his family became an enormous scandal. It exposed Yazid, destroyed the image surrounding him, and stripped his rule of legitimacy.

It also helped drive the Muslim community toward revolt.

History records several uprisings against Umayyad rule after the killing of Imam Husayn. Some were led by people devoted to Husayn, others by people with different motives, but many raised the banner of avenging his blood.

The Sufyani ruling house soon collapsed, only a few years after Husayn’s martyrdom.

The same was eventually true of the Marwanid branch of the Umayyad dynasty that followed it.

It fell to the Abbasid revolution, and one of the slogans the Abbasids used against Umayyad rule was:

> “Avenge Husayn!”

Many revolts arose against the Umayyads, and all of them drew in one way or another on the example of Husayn’s uprising: from the Tawwabun and al-Mukhtar, to Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr and Abdullah ibn al-Ashath, and later Zayd ibn Ali, the great martyr, followed by others all the way to the Abbasids.

So Yazid failed to achieve his central goals.

Yes, one of his aims did succeed: revenge.

He took revenge on the Prophet’s household and Companions by killing those they loved and leaving them in grief. He took revenge on the Muslims of Medina through the horrors we have described. And he took revenge on Mecca and its people by laying siege to the holy city and terrorizing its population.

But all of that passed.

And in the end, those acts produced consequences that worked against Yazid and in favor of Husayn’s goals.

Husayn, by contrast, remains to this day a symbol of the preservation of Islam.

His blood is still treated as a beacon: protecting Islam, awakening Muslims, and exposing the savage forces of ignorance that oppose the Prophet from within and without.

The effects and blessings of his uprising and his blood, in this view, remain visible in our own time.

Oppressed peoples have drawn inspiration from his example: during the Islamic Revolution in Iran, during the Israeli occupation of Lebanon, and today in the struggle against ISIS and other extremist groups of the same kind.

This way of thinking, this spirit, this will, this determination—all of it has been nourished by the blessing of Husayn’s blood, whose influence remains alive and powerful.

I want to end this part with one clear example that brings our discussion of worldly victory to a close.

By this point, it should be clear that, by worldly standards, Husayn was the victor.

When Imam Zayn al-Abidin returned to Medina after captivity, one of the people who met him was Ibrahim ibn Talha ibn Ubayd Allah, a well-known figure in the city.

He asked the Imam:

> “Who won?”

That question still works today, more than a thousand years after Husayn’s martyrdom.

Who won that battle?

Zayn al-Abidin’s answer, given only weeks after Husayn was killed, works just as well today.

It was only a few words, but it contains the whole argument we have just made:

> “When the time for prayer comes, give the call to prayer and the call to begin it. Then you will know who won.”

Because in reality, the struggle was about what the call to prayer itself proclaims.

As long as prayer remains, and as long as it remains joined to the name of the Prophet, every worshipper can know who won.

The victor was Husayn.

The one who was defeated—and whose defeat became clearer and clearer with time—was Yazid, together with his project and his goals.

## The Results in Terms of the Next Life

It is clear that, by the standards of the next life, Imam Husayn was the victor.

I will deal with this side of the question more briefly, because the main purpose here is to draw lessons from it. Let us look at the most important signs of Husayn’s victory in the next life.

The first is that throughout the whole movement that led to Karbala, Husayn was trying to fulfill **the religious duty God had placed upon him**.

He was trying to live as a true servant of God.

Husayn was wholly devoted to God. Anyone who reads his Prayer of Arafah can see something of the depth of that devotion.

Whatever God wanted, whatever pleased God, whatever God commanded, Husayn was prepared to do, whatever the cost.

That is what it truly means to fulfill one’s duty.

From the moment Yazid sent the order to the governor of Medina—take Husayn’s pledge of allegiance, and if he refuses, kill him—Husayn’s duty was to refuse allegiance to Yazid ibn Muawiya.

Yazid’s whole project could not tolerate the idea of Husayn refusing to recognize him.

From the very first moment, our Imam remained faithful to what he understood his duty to be.

And this question—**what is my duty?**—is something we should always ask ourselves.

In every situation, in every challenge:

What is my duty here?

The question is not: What do I want? What do I enjoy? What serves my personal interests?

The important thing is to understand what my real religious duty is.

We are servants of God in this world, and this world is a place of trial and testing. We were created here so that our actions would show who among us does best.

A person therefore has to ask:

What does God require of me?

What action will please Him?

Then one must commit to that duty and carry it out, regardless of what pleases one’s own desires, one’s relatives, one’s friends, one’s group, or anyone else.

The central goal is the pleasure of God.

Husayn therefore knew with certainty that his duty was **not to pledge allegiance to Yazid, whatever the cost**.

And that is where the story begins.

There was no force in Medina capable of protecting Husayn, standing beside him, and defending him. His next duty was therefore to leave the city.

This was the city of his fathers and forefathers, where he had been born and raised, where the grave of his grandfather, the Messenger of God, lay, and where the memory of his mother Fatima al-Zahra remained.

He left all of that behind and went to Mecca.

Mecca was a gathering place for Muslims arriving for Hajj and Umrah, so a new duty arose there: to inform people, explain the truth about Yazid, expose his project and aims, and warn them of the danger of his growing power.

Husayn did this through explanation, public statements, letters, and correspondence.

Then the people of Kufa sent word to him:

> “Come, grandson of the Messenger of God. You have an army ready for you in Kufa.”

At that point, his duty became to travel to Kufa, where supporters, helpers, and a popular base appeared ready to receive him and support his cause.

When he reached the area near Kufa and was forced toward Karbala, where he and his family and companions were surrounded, his duty remained to refuse allegiance even if that refusal led to fighting.

And when the Kufan army would not let him go unless he pledged allegiance—or else be killed—his duty became to fight in defense of himself, his family, and his children, while refusing a humiliating submission.

It ended in martyrdom.

So from the very beginning of the events—from the first demand that he pledge allegiance until the moment of his martyrdom—Husayn was fulfilling what he understood to be his God-given duty.

By the standards of the next life, that is a true victory.

One of the things that saves a person on the Day of Judgment is precisely this: to fulfill the duty God has placed upon them, to obey Him, and to remain faithful to what He has commanded, regardless of the worldly result.

We in the resistance, for example, concluded that our duty was to fight our enemy.

We are not responsible for the result.

Our responsibility is to remain faithful to the religious duty we have identified, which is to fight the enemy, whether or not the worldly goal is ultimately achieved.

The outcome is an outcome.

It lies outside the boundaries of our responsibility.

So by the standards of the next life, Husayn was the victor in this movement, because at every moment he was fulfilling his religious duty before God.

The second point is **the triumph of martyrdom**.

A person may spend an entire life faithfully carrying out their religious duties and yet not end that life as a martyr. That depends on the will of God.

To attain martyrdom is therefore, by the standards of the next life, itself a victory and a triumph.

This is the secret behind Imam Ali’s love of martyrdom and his constant longing for it.

Many statements attributed to him express this idea.

He said:

> “By God, the son of Abu Talib is more at home with death [being killed in the path of Almighty God] than a child at its mother’s breast.” [^part02-p019-n1]

And he said:

> “By the One in whose hand is Ali’s soul, a thousand blows from swords are easier for me than dying in bed.” [^part02-p019-n2]

And when Ibn Muljam attacked him in the Mosque of Kufa and struck his head with a sword, he said:

> “By the Lord of the Kaaba, I have triumphed.”

For Imam Ali, then, death as a martyr was itself a victory.

So what we have described leads ultimately to victory in the next life and to the blessings God has promised there. Think of the servant who fulfills the duty God has given him and whose life ends in martyrdom. What awaits him with God? What rank, honor, and dignity?

God promises believers and the righteous safety, peace, rest, reassurance, honor, blessing, high rank, and His pleasure in that eternal life. If this is what awaits believers in general, then what must await God’s devoted servants who die as martyrs?

When God speaks of what awaits the righteous, the believers, those who struggle in His cause, and the martyrs in the next life—and He does so in dozens of verses—He sometimes says:

> “That is the great triumph.” [^part03-p001-n1]

Elsewhere:

> “The clear triumph.” [^part03-p001-n2]

And elsewhere:

> “The supreme triumph.” [^part03-p001-n3]

There are many expressions like these. God Himself describes this triumph as great.

And that is Husayn’s destiny.

But where is Yazid?

Yazid followed his own desires. He obeyed his devil, defied his Lord, and died a wretched death. From the moment he entered the next world, his condition became like that of Pharaoh’s people:

> “And the worst punishment engulfed Pharaoh’s people: the Fire, to which they are exposed morning and evening.”

That is happening now, from the moment of their death—not on the Day of Resurrection, because there is no morning and evening on that Day.

Then:

> “And on the Day the Hour comes: ‘Admit Pharaoh’s people to the severest punishment.’” [^part03-p002-n1]

Some people will fight for power in this world and stop at nothing to get it. They will lie, backbite, spread gossip, stir up trouble, bribe people, and spend money just to become a village head, a mayor, a council member, an MP, a minister, a governor, a director, or a president.

But all of it disappears.

Read history. Look at the great kings whose empires once stretched across vast territories, and ask what remains of them and their kingdoms now.

The real kingdom is the kingdom of the next life.

God speaks about it in Surat al-Insan, when He describes what He has prepared for those who fed the orphan, the poor, and the captive, and how they will be rewarded in Paradise:

> “They will be waited on by immortal youths. If you saw them, you would think them scattered pearls. And wherever you looked, you would see bliss and a magnificent kingdom.” [^part03-p003-n1]

So if you want a kingdom, the kingdom is there in Paradise. If you want status, real status is there. If you want greatness, real greatness is there. If you want honor, real honor is there.

Power in this world, social standing in this world, authority in this world, greatness in this world, honor in this world—none of these things has lasting value. They disappear and come to an end. They are not worth committing sins to obtain, no matter how small those sins may seem.

So let me close this part of the discussion about the next life with this.

We belong to a religious tradition that teaches us that if we fulfill the duty God has given us, remain on His path, and work and struggle in His cause, then whatever happens to us in this world, what awaits us in the next is victory:

> “Say, ‘What are you waiting to happen to us except one of two good outcomes?’” [^part03-p004-n1]

Those two outcomes are martyrdom or victory.

Within this Islamic understanding of faith, there is no ultimate defeat. Victory is always the final outcome: a personal victory for the person who is martyred, and victory for the community if it accepts its responsibilities and carries them through. In that case, it will inevitably reap the fruits of victory.

## The Final Conclusion

Let us now return to the question with which we began this chapter.

Everything we have discussed confirms that Husayn was victorious at Ashura by both measures: the measure of this world and the measure of the next.

Yes, it was a victory made through blood, tears, pain, and grief. But the fragrance of Husayn still rises from that blood-soaked victory.

Imam Khomeini called it **“the victory of blood over the sword.”**

What does that mean?

It means that the aims for which the blood was shed were achieved, while the aims pursued by the sword failed.

And what we see in our own time confirms that blood defeated the sword in the year 61 AH, and that blood is still defeating the sword today: the blood of people who are oppressed, besieged, persecuted, isolated, and targeted by international and regional conspiracies, yet continue to fight through siege, isolation, and hardship in country after country, on front after front, and in field after field.

# Chapter Two

## Husayn’s Struggle: The Roots and Origins of the Conflict

In the first chapter, we explained what it means to say that Imam Husayn truly defeated Yazid, and we looked at every side of that victory in terms of both this world and the next.

In this chapter, we will go deeper into what happened at Karbala by looking at the conflict beneath the event itself.

That means widening our view.

We are no longer going to look only at the confrontation between Husayn and Yazid. We will look at the deeper struggle between truth and falsehood: where it began, what lies at its roots, and why it continues.

This struggle has existed on earth in every age. It existed before Karbala and after it. It exists in our own time, and it will continue until the end of time.

If we go back to its roots, we can better understand what this struggle really is, what lies behind it, and why it continues throughout human history. God speaks about it repeatedly in the Qur’an, across many different surahs.

So what is this struggle really about?

## Stage One: Understanding the Battle, Its Nature, and Its Arena

The Qur’an tells the story of Adam and Iblis in a number of places.

Adam represents the human being as such. Iblis, although he is one particular individual, represents the jinn.

When we say that the Qur’an tells us a story, we should remember what that means: **God Himself is telling it to us.** He is the one giving us the details of what happened.

Once we keep that in mind—that this story is being told to us by God through His enduring Book—we understand that the story must matter deeply.

And in this case, the story is about the conflict between Adam and Iblis.

It contains lessons and warnings with consequences for people in every age. The Qur’an is not simply a record of past events. Above all, it is a source of guidance for humanity.

The fact that the same story appears more than once in the Qur’an—sometimes in greater detail and sometimes more briefly—is another sign of its importance. It has a central role in guiding people, showing them the path, defining their responsibilities, and revealing the depth of the struggle they are part of.

That struggle will be our subject here.

We will begin with the event itself, then draw out its meaning and express it in the language of our own time.

## The Story of Adam and Iblis

Before God created Adam, the father of humanity, He had already created the heavens and the earth, the angels, the jinn, the animals, and the rest of creation. The angels worshipped God.

Among the jinn was a man [^part03-p011-n1] named Iblis.

Iblis was intensely devoted to worship. He prayed, bowed, prostrated himself, glorified God, and remembered Him constantly. Because of the extent of his worship, he came to be counted among the company of the angels and joined that blessed and holy assembly.

Then God created Adam.

God intended to place him on earth as His appointed steward:

> “I am placing a successor on earth.” [^part03-p012-n1]

God had not given this role to any angel or jinn. He chose the human being—Adam.

This was a sign of the honor and dignity God had given humanity.

He taught Adam the names of all things and showed the angels and the rest of creation why Adam had been given this distinction and why he was worthy of this role.

Then God commanded the angels to prostrate themselves before Adam.

This was not an act of worship. It was an act of respect and honor toward the one God had chosen and distinguished on earth.

> “So the angels all prostrated themselves together.” [^part03-p012-n2]

All except Iblis.

Iblis was not himself an angel, but had joined their company. He refused. He became proud and rebellious because there was pride inside him, vanity, selfishness, envy, and a sense of superiority based on his own origin.

He said to God:

> “I am better than him. You created me from fire, and You created him from clay.” [^part03-p013-n1]

That was the first rebellion.

As a result, God expelled this rebel from His mercy and cursed him. Iblis became accursed, banished, rejected, and condemned because he had defied the Lord of all worlds.

But Iblis asked God to extend his life until the Day of Resurrection:

> “He said, ‘My Lord, then give me respite until the day they are raised.’” [^part03-p013-n2]

God granted his request and allowed him to live until the appointed time.

God then told Adam and his wife to live in Paradise—whether this Paradise was in heaven or on earth is a separate question—and warned them that Iblis was their enemy.

There is an important point here.

From the very beginning of creation, from the first moments of humanity’s struggle with Iblis, God gave the human being two major advantages.

First, **He identified the enemy.**

God warned Adam and his wife—and through them all of Adam’s descendants—that Iblis was the enemy.

Second, **He revealed what the enemy wanted.**

He made Iblis’s aims and intentions clear.

These two things matter enormously. In any struggle—political, military, cultural, or otherwise—you need to know who your real enemy is. You need to understand what that enemy wants, what he intends, what his characteristics are, and what he is capable of. You must not become confused about any of this.

That knowledge is one of the foundations of victory.

We will now look more closely at both points through the verses of the Qur’an.

## Knowing the Enemy and Understanding His Goals

When Iblis rebelled against God’s will and command, and then asked God to let him live until the Day of Resurrection, he immediately and openly announced his objective.

He began his struggle by telling us exactly what he intended to do.

As we have said, when God speaks to us through the Qur’an, He is not simply telling us what happened. He is warning us, teaching us, making things clear to us, guiding us, and directing us.

First, He teaches us to recognize our bitter enemy.

Then He teaches us to recognize what that enemy wants.

God warns us about Iblis repeatedly in the Qur’an:

> “Satan is your enemy, so treat him as an enemy.” [^part03-p015-n1]

Elsewhere:

> “Satan is clearly an enemy to the human being.” [^part03-p015-n2]

And elsewhere:

> “Did I not command you, children of Adam, not to worship Satan? He is clearly your enemy.” [^part03-p015-n3]

There are many other verses in which God describes Satan as a fierce enemy of humanity.

So this is not a hidden enemy.

His hostility is clear. It has been openly declared.

Why, then, do people ignore his hostility? Why are they not prepared to confront him?

As for Iblis’s objective, it was—and remains—revenge against Adam and his descendants because of the honor God had given them.

It began with envy toward Adam and the pride that made Iblis refuse to prostrate before him. From there, it became a desire for revenge.

It is as though Iblis said to God:

Look at this one whom You have honored above me. This Adam whom You have preferred over me. I will show You what I will do with him.

The Qur’an gives us his words:

> “He said, ‘Do You see this one whom You have honored above me? If You give me until the Day of Resurrection—’” [^part03-p016-n1]

Listen carefully, children of Adam:

> “—I will surely bring his descendants under my control, except for a few.”

In other words: if You give me the time and opportunity, I will take control of them by leading them astray.

His intention was clear from the beginning.

God tells us elsewhere that Iblis said:

> “Because You have let me go astray, I will lie in wait for them on Your straight path.” [^part03-p017-n1]

In other words: I will set ambushes for them along the straight path that leads to You, so that I can stop them from reaching You.

That is why Iblis is the first and greatest robber on the road traveled by those seeking their Lord.

He continues:

> “Then I will come at them from in front of them and from behind them, from their right and from their left, and You will find most of them ungrateful.” [^part03-p017-n2]

If Iblis can bring a person all the way to outright disbelief—to the point that the person denies the existence of God—that is his greatest ambition.

But if he cannot achieve that, then at the very least he wants to make the person ungrateful for God’s blessings.

That ingratitude appears in disobedience to God and in denying His gifts.

This is what Iblis means when he says:

> “You will find most of them ungrateful.”

In other words: most of Adam’s children will not be grateful for Your blessings. They will disobey You.

God gives us a third statement from Iblis:

> “He said, ‘My Lord, because You have let me go astray, I will make things on earth seem beautiful to them and lead them all astray, except Your devoted servants among them.’” [^part03-p018-n1]

So Iblis declared war on Adam, his wife, and all their descendants until the end of time.

It was to be a war without truce.

He would fight it together with those who help him—his forces among both jinn and human beings, whom the Qur’an describes as devils among mankind and jinn.

And he stated his aim plainly.

He wants to block the children of Adam from reaching God.

He wants to mislead them, tempt them, corrupt their faith, their conduct, their morals, and their humanity, so that they lose the decent and dignified life God intended for them on earth and are driven into the Fire in the next life.

God has made all of these aims clear to the children of Adam in His Book.

And before them, He made the enemy’s aims clear to Adam himself.

It is as though God said to Adam:

Iblis is your enemy. These are his objectives. I have given him time until the Day of Resurrection. The struggle between you is open. Each of you has resources at his disposal. You have will, knowledge, and ability, and he too has will, knowledge, and ability.

From that moment, the struggle became real.

## Returning to the Conflict Between Adam and Iblis

We have said that God made Iblis’s ultimate objectives clear to Adam.

But God also told Adam the specific goal of Iblis’s very first attack.

He said:

> “When We said to the angels, ‘Prostrate yourselves before Adam,’ they all prostrated themselves except Iblis, who refused. So We said, ‘Adam, this is an enemy to you and your wife. Do not let him drive you both out of Paradise, or you will suffer.’” [^part03-p019-n1]

So the first operation in Iblis’s war against Adam and his descendants had one immediate objective: to get Adam and his wife expelled from Paradise.

God told Adam exactly what Iblis wanted and warned him.

It was as though He said:

Adam, I have placed you in Paradise. You may remain here and eat freely from all its blessings. Everything here is permitted to you except this one tree. Do not go near it. And beware of Iblis, your enemy, because he wants to drive you out of Paradise.

By warning Adam of Iblis’s exact objective, God had in principle deprived Iblis of the element of surprise. Adam had every reason to be alert, cautious, and extremely careful.

But as we all know, Adam lost this first round.

God says:

> “Then Satan whispered to him. He said, ‘Adam, shall I show you the tree of immortality and a kingdom that never fades?’” [^part03-p020-n1]

And elsewhere:

> “But Satan caused them both to slip and drove them out of the state they had been in.” [^part03-p020-n2]

Some scholars have understood this first stage as a period of preparation and training for Adam and his wife.

And as a result of Iblis’s victory over Adam in that first encounter—and as we said earlier, we can properly describe it as the first victory Iblis won in his struggle with Adam and his descendants—the punishment was Adam’s expulsion from Paradise.

Yes. Adam wept, regretted what he had done, and repented. God accepted his repentance and chose him. But the consequences of that first defeat had already taken effect. The greatest of them was God’s decision that Adam, his wife, and Iblis should all descend to the earth:

> “We said, ‘Go down from here, all of you.’” [^part04-p001-n1]

And:

> “You will be enemies to one another.” [^part04-p001-n2]

With that, a new stage of the struggle began. It continues to this day, and it will continue until the Day of Resurrection.

Life on earth was not going to be like life in the Garden. Adam and his wife now had to live with the consequences of that transition. Here, human beings have responsibilities. They have to work, plant, harvest, face hunger and thirst and storms, and make use of the natural world to meet their needs. They also have duties of worship, alongside the struggle against Iblis and his forces, and much else besides.

On this earth, Adam and his descendants would have to bear responsibility. Here they would live and die. Here they would be buried. And from this earth they would be raised again on the Day of Resurrection to face judgment.

God says:

> “He said, ‘Go down. You will be enemies to one another. On earth you will have a place to live and provision for a time.’” [^part04-p002-n1]

That means until the Day of Resurrection.

He continues:

> “There you will live, there you will die, and from there you will be brought forth.” [^part04-p002-n2]

That is the day of resurrection, judgment, and reckoning.

Elsewhere, God describes the sequence in these words:

> “We said, ‘Go down from here, all of you. When guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows My guidance will have nothing to fear, nor will they grieve. But those who reject faith and deny Our signs will be the people of the Fire. There they will remain.’” [^part04-p002-n3]

Adam and his wife, of course, had certain protections that we do not need to discuss here. So Iblis increasingly turned his attention to Adam’s children and descendants. Earth was now the place where human life would multiply and generations would continue.

And this brings us to the first event in the struggle between Iblis and the children of Adam on earth: a brother killing his own brother.

Tradition identifies the two brothers as Cain and Abel. The Qur’an itself does not name them, but simply calls them “the two sons of Adam”:

> “Tell them the true story of Adam’s two sons. Each offered a sacrifice. It was accepted from one but not from the other. One said, ‘I will kill you.’ The other replied, ‘God accepts only from those who are mindful of Him.’” [^part04-p003-n1]

The story is well known.

Abel, who was righteous, pure, and devout, offered a sacrifice to God. We do not need to enter here into the question of what exactly the sacrifice was for.

Cain, who was envious, selfish, arrogant, and corrupt, also offered a sacrifice.

God accepted Abel’s offering, because:

> “God accepts only from those who are mindful of Him.”

Cain became angry and refused to accept the outcome. He threatened to kill his brother, and then:

> “His own soul made the killing of his brother seem easy to him, so he killed him.” [^part04-p003-n2]

Abel thus became the first innocent martyr to be killed unjustly.

When we read this story, we should once again notice the role of envy, along with the whispering of Iblis.

As we have already seen, envy was one of the causes of the first conflict in heaven.

And here, envy becomes one of the causes of the first conflict on earth.

So no one should ever make light of this filthy, satanic disease of the heart.

Cain, as we have seen, became the first of Adam’s descendants to serve as a soldier for Iblis. His own corruption and injustice led him down the path of crime, tyranny, and destruction.

And with that, two camps emerged.

This was the first division within humanity, the first human conflict, and the first clash that ended in killing. From then on, two camps existed on earth. They have been in conflict ever since, and they will remain so until the Day of Resurrection.

This is the deeper meaning of the story.

The first camp is the camp of Adam, his wife, and the righteous among their descendants. Its leaders are the prophets and those who follow them, from Adam all the way to the final Prophet.

The second is the camp of Iblis and his forces among both human beings and jinn.

## Summary

We have now seen that there are two camps and two opposing projects, locked in a struggle that continues through history.

Each camp has its own goals, and each has fought across the generations to achieve them.

If we look at all the different forms this struggle has taken over time, we find that the conflicts between human beings are, at their deepest level, renewed versions of that first struggle between Adam and Iblis.

The real battle was not simply between Abel and Cain. It was between Abel and Iblis.

The battle was not really between Noah and the arrogant leaders of his people. Those men had handed themselves over to Iblis. They had surrendered their minds, their wills, and their leadership to him.

The same was true of Abraham and Nimrod. At the deepest level, the conflict was between Abraham and Iblis, while Nimrod was merely an instrument in Iblis’s hands.

The same applies to Moses and Pharaoh; to Jesus and the tyrants of his time; and to the Prophet Muhammad and Abu Jahl and the tyrants of Quraysh.

The real struggle was with Iblis.

Abu Jahl was a soldier, an officer, perhaps even a commander in Iblis’s army. But the one directing the camp, making the plans, and whispering in people’s minds was Iblis himself.

This conflict between the two camps and their two projects will continue to the end.

Iblis’s camp wants the individual human being, and humanity as a whole, to go astray: to turn away from God, deny the Creator, reject His blessings, commit injustice, become tyrannical, spread corruption, and commit crimes. The result is misery in this life and punishment in the next.

That is the whole purpose of Iblis’s project.

It is the same project we have seen at work from the time of Cain until our own generation.

Opposing it is the camp of the prophets and those who follow them. Their aim is for individual human beings, and humanity as a whole, to know their Lord, believe in Him, be grateful for His blessings, obey His commands, and worship Him on this earth, so that they may live a good, honorable, dignified life here and enjoy everlasting happiness in the next life.

These, then, are the goals of the two camps.

Everything that happens in this struggle is a particular expression of that larger conflict. The generations change. The times change. The people change. But the basic struggle remains the same.

It is the struggle that began when God commanded the angels to bow before Adam.

So the main conclusion of this first stage is clear.

We now know the true nature of the battle.

And we also know where it is being fought.

**The battlefield is the human being.**

That means the individual human being, and humanity as a whole.

People themselves are the ground over which these two camps struggle.

The prophets want people to become true servants of the Lord of all, and to attain goodness and happiness in this life and the next.

Iblis wants hell, misery, crime, corruption, oppression, tyranny, turmoil, error, and moral collapse for them.

So the arena of the struggle is humanity itself.

**The battlefield is the human being.**

## Stage Two: The Battle for the Human Being

Once we know the enemy and understand his goals, which was the first stage of our discussion, we have to move to the second.

Now we need to understand the enemy’s abilities.

What can he do?

What methods does he use?

What tools and tactics does he rely on?

Where is he strong and where is he weak?

How long can he persist?

At the same time, human beings need to understand their own abilities as well: their strengths and weaknesses, their flaws and vulnerable points, how secure their defenses are, and where the enemy may be able to enter.

This is essential in any conflict.

Whatever kind of battle you are fighting, you need to know your enemy, his plans, and his capabilities. And you also need to know yourself and your own side.

Anyone who enters a battle without knowing what the enemy can do, what his plans are, or what circumstances he is operating in—and who at the same time does not understand his own resources, how many fighters he has, how much ammunition and weaponry he possesses, how long he can hold out, or how long the people around him can endure—is heading toward disaster.

The same applies to this greater struggle between the two camps we have been discussing.

Human beings enter this battle with their whole selves. They are facing a relentless enemy. So they need to know what that enemy is capable of.

Iblis has many abilities.

But human beings have abilities too.

If God had not given human beings the means to defeat Iblis and his forces and frustrate their plans, He would not have commanded us to confront them, defeat them, refuse to surrender to them, and refuse to hand over our minds, hearts, faith, and selves to them.

A nine-year-old girl, once she reaches the age of religious responsibility, is expected to fulfill her duty in this struggle.

A fifteen-year-old boy is expected to do the same.

Would God place a young boy or girl in a struggle against Iblis without giving them the ability to defeat him?

Of course not.

A young boy or girl—let alone an adult man or woman—is capable of defeating Iblis and his entire project, provided the necessary conditions are met.

So, since we have said that knowing the enemy’s abilities is essential, let us now look at what Iblis is actually capable of.

## Iblis and His Capabilities

The first thing to understand about Iblis is the extent of his knowledge.

Iblis, the enemy of humanity, possesses immense knowledge.

According to some traditions, he worshiped God alongside the angels for thousands of years before Adam was created. He witnessed the angels and the unseen realm of the heavens and the earth. He saw the Garden. He witnessed the creation of Adam. And he has witnessed everything that has happened from the time Adam was created until today.

Someone who has lived for thousands of years and is still alive will naturally possess an enormous store of knowledge, experience, and practical understanding.

Beyond that, Iblis is an expert in what we would now call psychological warfare.

That follows from the first point.

He has watched human beings from the beginning. He witnessed their creation. He has dealt with generation after generation and nation after nation. He knows every kind of person, and he has watched every period of human history unfold.

All of this gives him an extraordinary understanding of the human mind.

And someone who understands the human mind that well will naturally become skilled at manipulating it.

Human beings, whether men or women, are naturally drawn to some things and repelled by others.

People love money.

They love power and status.

They enjoy praise and admiration.

They are drawn to whatever seems beautiful to them.

They want comfort and ease. If they could live without hardship, exhaustion, or sacrifice, most people would gladly choose that. If they could have everything they wanted, they would be happy to take it.

Human beings therefore have physical, social, emotional, and psychological needs.

And this is the important point:

**Iblis knows those needs.**

He also knows that most people have weaknesses, especially when the right conditions bring those weaknesses to the surface—envy, anger, and other vices of the same kind.

Because he knows the human mind in such detail, Iblis is constantly studying us.

You could say that he builds a personal file on every human being, recording the weaknesses he can use as points of attack.

This is why I remind myself, and all believers, that we must never forget what Iblis is capable of.

Our struggle with him does not happen once a year, once a month, or even once a day.

It continues hour by hour, minute by minute, second by second—with every breath we take.

It begins when a person becomes religiously responsible and continues until the soul leaves the body.

So, in summary, Iblis has two major advantages.

The first is the enormous amount of information he possesses about us and the circumstances of our lives.

The second is his deep experience of human psychology: he knows how to approach different people in different ways, speaking to each person in the language they understand and respond to, according to their particular needs.

He uses these abilities to enter into each of us and gain control over our minds, hearts, wills, decisions, and behavior.

But now we come to the most important question:

**How much power does Iblis really have?**

Despite everything we have said, Iblis is still a weak creature.

God says:

> “The schemes of Satan are weak.” [^part04-p013-n1]

So if that is true, where does his strength come from?

Has God given him some direct power over creation, some authority by which he can simply control other beings?

No.

God has given him no such power.

Iblis is weak. His real tools are temptation and suggestion.

He takes something worthless or ugly and makes it look beautiful.

And in doing this, he uses his knowledge of laws, religious teachings, doctrines, and schools of thought.

He approaches a religious scholar as a religious scholar.

He approaches a devout person through the language of devotion.

He approaches a drug dealer through the mentality of a drug dealer.

He approaches a criminal through the mentality of a criminal.

He understands the weaknesses of each kind of person.

He also knows how people use religious rulings, legal arguments, technical loopholes, tricks, evasions, and lies to justify what they want to do.

Iblis can make almost anything look attractive.

History gives us countless examples.

Take idol worship.

How could a rational person worship a stone?

The people who first turned toward idols surely knew that the idol itself was only stone.

So Iblis whispered to them:

This stone is only a symbol of God. You are not really worshiping the stone. You are worshiping God through it.

That may have seemed reasonable enough at first.

But over time, the symbol itself became an object of worship.

That is how Iblis works.

He moves gradually.

He works in stages.

He makes use of everything he knows in order to whisper, tempt, and mislead.

So what does Iblis actually do?

He makes things attractive.

He fills people with false hopes.

He makes promises.

He encourages them to rationalize.

He tells them to put things off.

He makes them forget.

**That is the true extent of his power.**

Returning to the original story, God says:

> “Satan whispered to them.” [^part04-p015-n1]

When Iblis caused Adam and his wife to leave the Garden, he did not use some supernatural force to compel them.

He did not threaten them with a weapon.

He did not bribe them with money.

He deceived them.

Adam believed the deception.

That was the whole method: Iblis whispered to him.

## What Is Required of Us

So we can now see that Iblis has no absolute power over human beings.

Some people try to excuse their weakness by saying: God has set Iblis and the devils loose against us, and we have no power to resist them.

But that is not true.

Iblis only gains power over those who give it to him—those who open their ears and hearts to him until they become obedient servants of him and his project.

A human being must understand the limits of Iblis’s power.

Each of us should be able to say:

I am a servant of God, not a servant of Iblis.

I am a human being, and I have dignity.

I am a child of Adam—the one before whom God once commanded Iblis to bow as an act of honor and respect.

How, then, could I ever become Iblis’s slave, submit to him, and obey his commands?

God says:

> “You will have no authority over My servants, except those who follow you among the misguided.” [^part04-p016-n1]

In other words, a person who follows Iblis places himself under Iblis’s authority by doing so.

Anyone who wants to remain a servant of God alone can do that—provided that he does not follow Iblis and his temptations.

God says elsewhere:

> “When you recite the Qur’an, seek refuge in God from Satan, the accursed. He has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord. His authority is only over those who take him as an ally and those who associate others with God.” [^part04-p016-n2]

And in another verse, Satan himself says after everything is over:

> “Satan will say, when the matter has been decided: ‘God made you a true promise, and I made you a promise and broke it. I had no authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I cannot save you, and you cannot save me. I reject the way you once associated me with God. The wrongdoers will have a painful punishment.’” [^part04-p017-n1]

We end this chapter, then, with a set of questions.

Can we confront Iblis, despite all the advantages he possesses?

What abilities has God given us that make us responsible for entering this struggle and winning it?

In some battles, we are required only to fight. Victory is something we hope for, but it is not itself the duty placed on us.

The battle with Iblis is different.

Here, we are required not merely to fight.

**We are required to win.**

We are required to defeat Iblis.

So what has God given us that makes that victory possible?

That is the question we will answer in Chapter Three, God willing.

# Chapter Three

## How Iblis Works and How We Can Resist Him

In this chapter, we will pick up where we left off at the end of the previous one. But before we do, there is an important point about the story of Adam and Iblis that we should address.

## Adam and Eve: Who Was Responsible?

Our purpose in discussing that story was to understand the heart of the struggle between human beings and their enemy, Iblis—a struggle that began at that moment.

But the story itself raises many other questions and contains many details, lessons, and points of reflection that would require much longer studies. That was not our purpose here.

People may ask, for example: Was the garden where Adam and his wife lived, and from which they were later expelled, in heaven or on earth? If it was in heaven, was it the final Paradise to which believers will be admitted, or some other garden?

How long did Adam and Eve remain there before they were expelled? What was the tree that God forbade Adam to eat from, or even approach? What exactly did Iblis do during that episode?

Many such questions are commonly raised. Anyone who wants to explore them in detail can find discussions in works of Qur’anic commentary, theology, and the stories of the prophets.

But leaving all of that aside, there is one point connected to our discussion that I want to address because it corrects a widespread misunderstanding in popular culture.

Many people wrongly accuse Eve, the wife of the Prophet Adam, of being the one who persuaded Adam to eat from the tree. According to this view, she bears full responsibility for what happened at the beginning of this struggle. In other words, people blame their own mother Eve for being the cause of the sin.

This idea is very old. It goes back to times before the Prophet Muhammad. It developed among some of the Children of Israel and later spread among Muslims through Jews living in the Arabian Peninsula. In this way, the idea entered the culture of some Muslims.

But from the standpoint of Islamic teaching, it has no sound basis.

The Qur’anic passages about Adam and Eve say nothing of the kind. A quick look at the verses is enough to show this.

God says:

> “Then Satan whispered to him and said: ‘Adam, shall I show you the tree of immortality and a kingdom that never fades?’” [^part05-p006-n1]

The verse explicitly says that Iblis whispered to Adam, not to Eve. Adam was the focus of his efforts and the direct target of his temptation.

Other verses, however, indicate that both Adam and Eve were subjected to Iblis’s whispering and deception. God says:

> “Then Satan whispered to them both.” [^part05-p006-n2]

And, describing how God reproached them both:

> “Their Lord called to them: ‘Did I not forbid you both from that tree and tell you that Satan was a clear enemy to you?’” [^part05-p007-n1]

And elsewhere:

> “Then Satan caused them both to slip from it and brought them out of the state they had been in.” [^part05-p007-n2]

At most, these verses tell us that responsibility was shared.

Adam had his freedom, his judgment, and his own independent decision. Eve also had her freedom, her judgment, and her own independent will. They made the decision together and acted together.

So if we read all these Qur’anic passages together, the conclusion is that Adam and Eve shared responsibility for what happened. One could even understand the verses as placing the greater responsibility on Adam.

But the point is not to shift the blame onto Adam.

The point is to remove from people’s minds this false idea that our mother Eve was responsible for our father Adam being expelled from Paradise—and, by extension, that women are somehow to blame for corruption in the world in general.

## Iblis: His Message and His Capabilities

Returning to our main discussion, we concluded in the previous chapter that Iblis’s goal is to mislead people and turn them away from God.

We also said that he has no power over human beings unless they follow him, obey him, respond to him, and give him power over themselves.

This is an important point and deserves emphasis.

God says:

> “And Satan will say, once the matter has been decided: ‘God made you a true promise, while I also made you a promise and then broke it. I had no authority over you. I only called you, and you answered me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I cannot save you, and you cannot save me. I reject the way you once associated me with God. The wrongdoers will have a painful punishment.’” [^part05-p009-n1]

God had already warned people what would follow if they obeyed Him and what would follow if they disobeyed Him.

We also said that Iblis possesses enormous knowledge, experience, and skill. He has countless methods, tools, and tactics at his disposal in his effort to mislead people.

But the basic power he relies on is much simpler.

He whispers. He makes wrongdoing look attractive. He supplies excuses. He makes promises. He holds out false hopes. He encourages delay. He deceives and lies.

All his knowledge and experience would be useless without these methods. And even these methods only work if a human being responds to them.

This is why no one should excuse their surrender to Iblis by saying that he possesses overwhelming or supernatural powers.

His first target in this struggle is **us as individuals**.

He works on each of us separately. He tries to gain control over our mind, our heart, our will, and our decisions, until a person becomes his follower and ally—and eventually an obedient servant.

The Qur’an speaks repeatedly in these terms.

## What Helps Iblis

We have seen that Iblis has many abilities and methods that he uses in his struggle against the children of Adam.

But he also has something else helping him.

And unfortunately, it is inside us.

This makes the situation much more dangerous.

To use a military comparison, imagine an army trying to capture a city, fortress, or castle. It has people inside the fortress helping it. They pass information to the attacking army, tell it where the weak points are, and open back doors so that soldiers can slip inside.

That is obviously a very dangerous situation.

If the fortress were well defended and there were no agents inside it, the situation would be very different.

Something similar happens in the human struggle with Iblis.

Unfortunately, Iblis has an ally inside the human being.

Islamic teaching calls it **“the self that urges us toward evil.”**

God created us with desires, instincts, and needs. These are natural parts of every human being.

Without them, people would not naturally seek food. They would not defend themselves, because there would be no instinct for survival. The human race would not continue, because there would be no sexual and reproductive instinct. The same is true of many other basic human drives.

So God placed these desires within us for our good, not to harm us.

They are meant to help us live well and find happiness in this life and the next.

But the same desires and needs that can become a source of good can also become a source of disaster.

Most human abilities work this way.

Take scientific knowledge. Physics, for example, can be used to improve human life through nuclear energy, medical technology, or ways of dealing with environmental problems. But the same knowledge can also be used to kill people.

Fire can cook food, or it can burn down homes and kill people.

A weapon can defend someone who is being oppressed, or it can be used to kill the innocent.

The same human ability can therefore be used in very different ways, depending on the purpose of the person using it.

Our desires are no different.

This is where Iblis enters the picture.

He exploits the desires already present within us: the desire for food, the instinct for survival, and many others.

And survival is not quite the same thing as loving life.

The instinct to survive can make someone cling to life even in humiliation and submission.

Karbala is one example. People feared for themselves and their children, and that fear helped lead them into a conspiracy that ended in the most terrible crime in history.

Iblis uses these instincts because they give him something to work with. They prepare the ground, open doors and windows for him, and make his task easier.

So in this struggle, every one of us faces an alliance between two forces.

The first is Iblis and his devils.

The second is the self that urges us toward evil, together with uncontrolled desires.

These two work together against us and try to draw us onto a path different from the one God wants for us.

This is why the Qur’an, the Prophet, and the teachings attributed to the Prophet’s family speak repeatedly about worshipping Satan, following Satan, obeying Satan, and taking Satan as an ally.

At the same time, they also warn against blindly following one’s own desires—the impulses of the self that urges us toward evil.

Islamic teaching contains repeated warnings against both.

God says:

> “Have you seen the one who takes his own desire as his god?” [^part05-p014-n1]

To make one’s own desire into a god besides God is a very clear image of what it means to obey desire and follow one’s appetites.

God also says:

> “But the wrongdoers followed their desires without knowledge.” [^part05-p014-n2]

When a person follows the part of the self that urges toward evil, follows every desire, abandons reason, and gives himself over to every impulse and craving, he becomes caught in Satan’s traps.

This is also why, in religious prayers, we find people asking God to help them against both their own lower self and their Satan.

Once we understand this, we can understand the saying found in some traditions:

> “Your worst enemy is the self within you.”

This does not mean that the human self as such is the enemy.

It means the part of the self that urges us toward evil and pushes our desires beyond the limits set by God.

Now that we have explained the role of Iblis and the way he works through this part of the human self, it will help to look at some ordinary experiences from everyday life.

## How Iblis Works in Everyday Life

God speaks in the Qur’an about Iblis and his forces among the jinn.

The prophets also spoke about him, and some traditions even say that certain prophets saw him and spoke with him.

But we ourselves cannot see Iblis with our eyes or hear him with our ears. We cannot see his followers among the jinn either.

So we can ask a question.

Leaving aside faith itself, is there anything in ordinary human experience that allows us to sense the presence of Iblis in a more direct way?

The answer, I would say, can be found in experiences all of us already have.

After all, every one of us is still living in the middle of this struggle with Iblis and with the part of ourselves that urges us toward evil.

One of the clearest examples is what we normally call **talking to ourselves**.

All of us have moments when we feel as though we are having a conversation inside our own mind.

What is really happening, in this account, is a mixture of our own lower impulses and the whispering and embellishment of Iblis.

What we think of as a conversation with ourselves is, in reality, a conversation with Iblis—a dialogue, an argument, and a struggle.

The Qur’an describes its setting as the human breast:

> “From the evil of the whisperer who withdraws, who whispers into the hearts of people.” [^part05-p016-n1]

This does not necessarily mean the physical chest. It is a way of speaking about the inner human self.

Satan enters into the human self as blood runs through the veins.

We do not feel the blood moving through our veins. But we know that it is there because we are alive, conscious, and able to move our hands.

We know the blood is flowing because we see its effects.

In the same way, the argument here is that we recognize the activity of Iblis through its effects: the inner whispers, temptations, and rationalizations that appear in our minds.

Some examples may make the idea clearer.

Think of a believer during winter.

It is cold. Dawn has arrived, and it is time for the obligatory prayer.

Iblis comes and begins to whisper, trying to stop the person from getting out of bed.

The inner voice sounds something like this:

Stay where you are. It is warm here. Missing the dawn prayer just once is not a disaster. If you get up now, you will be tired at work. You can always make up the prayer later with the noon and afternoon prayers.

Suppose the person answers:

But prayer is important. God will hold me accountable for it.

Then comes another answer:

God is forgiving and merciful. God accepts repentance. Does not the tradition say that God loves the young person who repents? You are still young. You can always repent later.

This is the kind of inner conversation many people experience around the dawn prayer.

And how many people have listened to that voice, gone back to sleep, and missed the prayer?

This is an example of what is meant by Satan’s whispering.

Another example comes after someone has already missed the dawn prayer and intends to make it up during the day.

A voice inside says:

You do not have time now. People are waiting for you. You have work to do. Just pray the noon and afternoon prayers and make up the dawn prayer later—or tomorrow.

So the missed prayer is postponed again.

Then another one follows.

The backlog begins to grow.

This too is something many people recognize from their own lives.

Another example concerns young people.

It is especially important to speak to the young, though older people may be in even greater danger.

Iblis keeps telling them:

You have only just begun your youth. Are you really going to spend it praying, fasting, struggling, fighting, and thinking about martyrdom? Why the hurry?

You are fifteen years old. Wait until you are twenty-five.

Enjoy yourself. Have fun. Make the most of being young. Do whatever you feel like doing.

Then, ten years from now, you can sort yourself out, repent to God, join the ranks of those who struggle in His cause, worship Him, and make use of every opportunity.

Some people listen to this voice.

Others do not.

Consider another example.

A person is alone in a room with a large sum of money.

Perhaps it belongs to the company he works for, an institution, or an organization he belongs to. The details do not matter.

He is short of money himself.

Then the thought comes:

What harm would it really do if you took a thousand dollars? The organization will hardly notice. You need it.

And anyway, you are not stealing it.

Just think of it as a loan.

Write yourself a note saying that you owe the organization a thousand dollars, and when your situation improves, quietly put the money back. No one ever needs to know.

So he takes the money while telling himself that it is a loan.

He does not even think of himself as a thief.

Later he delays paying it back.

Then he delays again.

He even finds a religious excuse: if a debtor is in difficulty, payment can be postponed until things improve.

The voice says:

You are genuinely struggling financially. You cannot pay it back now. Leave it until later.

And little by little, the “loan” becomes theft.

The reader may already have noticed that some of these examples sound familiar.

Perhaps you have lived through something similar yourself.

Things like this happen every day, every hour.

All of these forms of inner reasoning—the delays, excuses, false hopes, temptations, and attempts to make wrongdoing look harmless—are, in this account, part of the work of Iblis.

Here is another useful example.

Imagine someone with a very strong spirit of sacrifice, someone ready to give almost anything for God’s sake.

Iblis comes to him and says:

Does the future of humanity and the good of the whole community really depend on **your** sacrifices?

Why should you be the one to sacrifice?

Why not this person or that person?

Haven’t you already given enough?

And now you are thinking of sending your own children to struggle and sacrifice too?

Why does that other man not send **his** children?

This kind of argument may begin to weaken a person’s willingness to sacrifice.

He accepts the reasoning and keeps his children away from the struggle.

Then Iblis whispers the same thing to someone else.

And that person does the same.

This is what happened at Karbala.

Each person thought that if he kept his own sons, brothers, and relatives away from fighting the army of Ibn Ziyad, it would make no real difference to the balance of the struggle.

Muslim ibn Aqil had fifteen thousand fighters.

If three men left, what difference could three make?

But then another three left.

And another three.

And so it continued until Muslim was left completely alone, with no one to support or defend him.

## What Makes Iblis’s Message Persuasive

Iblis’s efforts to mislead people have several features that make his message powerful and persuasive. The most important of these are the following:

1. **A logical argument:** As the examples we have already seen make clear, Iblis speaks to us in a way that *sounds* reasonable. He builds his case step by step, using a chain of arguments that appears logical and convincing.

2. **Making the argument attractive:** Iblis does not simply plant an idea in the heart. He dresses it up in the most appealing language he can, so that we are comfortable accepting it and persuading ourselves that it makes sense. He makes sin seem smaller than it is. He softens it, beautifies it, and convinces us that this particular act could never send us to Hell. His reassurance is always the same: God will forgive us and have mercy on us on the Day of Judgment. Sometimes he will even use verses of the Qur’an itself to lead a person astray.

3. **Relying on popular false beliefs:** Part of the strength of Iblis’s argument comes from his ability to use false ideas that are already widely accepted among people.[^part06-p001-n1] These make it easier for people to excuse sin.

Take the common idea that if someone has a good heart, God will forgive whatever they do simply because their heart is good. Iblis can start from that assumption and convince a person that there is no great harm in committing some smaller sin. After all, God can easily forgive something like that, especially if you are not cruel or malicious.

The same idea can then be used to excuse almost anything another person does: *He stole, but he has a good heart. He killed someone, but he has a good heart.*

People begin to imagine that having a good heart will somehow prevent God from holding them accountable. And in this way, sin is normalized and corruption spreads through society.

4. **Taking advantage of opportunities and weaknesses:** Iblis uses every opportunity that can help him lead us astray, and every weakness he can find in us. Some traditions use the expression **“the opportunities of Satan.”** The idea is that certain traits, or certain situations a person finds themselves in, can give Iblis exactly the opening he needs.

The traditions also speak of **“the snares of Iblis.”** These are the different things he uses to draw people in, and they are not the same for everyone.

The fewer weaknesses a person has, the more effort Iblis has to make. The more weaknesses a person has, the less effort he needs.

Imagine someone trying to drag different objects using ropes.

If he wants to pull a small stone, one rope is enough. But if he wants to move a huge, heavy boulder, he may need many ropes, with a different person pulling each one. The larger and heavier the object, the more ropes and people he needs.

Iblis deals with people in much the same way.

Some people are like grains of sand. He barely has to blow on them and they move.

Others are like stones. He needs a rope or two.

Some are like enormous boulders and require great effort.

And some are like firmly rooted mountains. No matter how hard he pulls, he cannot move them.

The difference lies in the number and seriousness of the weaknesses a person has.

It is useful here to look at a few examples of human weakness and how Iblis exploits them.

**First example: anger.**

When anger takes control of a person, it can give Iblis almost complete control over them.

Experience shows us that intense anger can make a person lose their judgment. They may swear at someone, insult them, lie, or even go as far as killing another human being—sometimes a relative, a child, or a spouse. We hear reports of such things every day.

And depending on a person’s power and position, anger can have consequences far beyond the individual. Some people, when angry, may even start a regional or international war.

Anger is therefore one of the most dangerous weaknesses a person can have. Imam Ali warned:

> “Beware of anger, for it is a mighty army among the armies of Iblis.” [^part06-p004-n1]

Anger is not described here as a single soldier or some minor force. It is described as an enormous army at Iblis’s disposal.

**Second example: self-admiration.**

When a person becomes impressed with themselves, they stop seeing their own faults. They begin to think they are better than everyone else, and praise becomes something they constantly crave.

This is a clear sign that Iblis has found a way in. Self-admiration begins to shape the person’s relationships, positions, words, judgments, and decisions.

Imam Ali therefore said:

> “Beware of admiring yourself, trusting in what pleases you about yourself, and loving praise. These are among Satan’s surest opportunities.” [^part06-p005-n1]

This is not merely an opportunity for Satan. It is one of his most reliable opportunities.

**Third example.**

A tradition tells of a conversation between Moses and Iblis. Moses asked him when, where, and how he gains power over a person. Among the answers attributed to Iblis was:

> “Moses, do not be alone with a woman who is not lawful for you. Whenever a man is alone with such a woman, I attend to him myself rather than sending one of my companions.” [^part06-p005-n2]

In some situations, Iblis sends one of his followers to do the work. But in other situations, he does not trust the task to anyone else.

When he sees an especially important opportunity—one that he believes may allow him to gain deep control over a person—he does not want to risk losing it. He takes charge himself.

That is the meaning of *“I attend to him myself.”*

One of the situations described in this way is a man and woman who are not permitted to be alone together spending time in private.

5. **A gradual and systematic approach:** This is another dangerous feature of the way Iblis works. He does not always look for immediate results. He can be extremely patient. He works on a person slowly, one step at a time.

Imam Ali is reported to have said:

> “He wants to undo your religion knot by knot.” [^part06-p006-n1]

In other words, Iblis works gradually. He does not always try to defeat a person in one dramatic attack. He first wins a few smaller battles, and eventually reaches the larger victory he wanted from the beginning.

This gradual method also means beginning with easy targets and moving slowly toward harder ones.

Iblis knows perfectly well, for example, that he cannot simply approach a committed believer and say, *Stop praying.* The person will almost certainly refuse.

Nor is he likely to begin by telling a committed Muslim to stop fasting in Ramadan. That is too difficult an objective at the start.

But he may find it much easier to lead the same believer into sins such as backbiting, gossip, or assuming the worst about other people.

So he begins with the easy targets.

This brings us back to what we said about each person’s weaknesses. Iblis uses them because they are the easiest points of entry. He avoids the strong parts of a person’s character and approaches through the weak ones.

An army trying to enter a fortified area does the same thing. It does not attack where there are bunkers, snipers, and heavy weapons if it can avoid them. It looks for the weak point—the gap that is easiest to break through.

Iblis does the same. Once he has entered through a weakness, he begins undoing the harder knots one by one.

Imagine a young man who is devout, conscientious, kind to people, and always ready to help others.

One evening, he is sitting with a group of young men who use drugs. One of them offers him a pill.

Iblis begins his work:

*One pill will not hurt you. You should try everything once.*

So the young man takes it.

That is the beginning.

Over time, he becomes addicted. Then one day he needs the drug but cannot afford it. Satan pushes him toward theft.

After a while, he becomes part of a group of thieves. Then, during some later incident, he finds himself killing someone in the course of a robbery.

So the path runs from one drug pill, to addiction, to theft, to joining a criminal gang, and finally to killing an innocent person.

Iblis has taken a devout and decent young man all the way to deliberate killing, beginning with nothing more than the suggestion that he try a single pill.

And these pills, unfortunately, can now be found throughout Lebanon—in schools, cities, towns, villages, even small villages.

That is how Iblis leads a person astray: **step by step.**

There is another example in a tradition attributed to Imam Ali.

A man had withdrawn from society to worship in a monastery, as some devout ascetics did in earlier times.

There was also a woman who had several brothers and was suffering from some kind of affliction. People in earlier times strongly believed that human beings could come under the influence of jinn or evil spirits. Her brothers therefore decided to take her to this worshipper in the hope that he might help her.

They brought her to him.

The man’s own desires made the situation attractive to him, and he slept with her.

Now he had committed the first sin.

Then Satan came to him.

*What are you going to do now? If her brothers find out, you will be disgraced in front of everyone.*

Then came the next suggestion:

*Kill her. If they discover what you have done, you will be ruined.*

The worshipper became more afraid of public shame than of the consequences of murder. With this argument, Satan made killing another human being seem acceptable to him.

So he killed her and buried her.

Her brothers came back, took hold of him, and went with him, trying to find out what had happened and where their sister was.

As they were walking, Satan came to him again and said:

*I am the one who made all of this seem attractive to you. Bow down to me. Just as your Lord once commanded me to bow before your father Adam, bow once before me and I will save you.*

And he did.

This man, an ascetic worshipper whose forehead had become accustomed to bowing before God, bowed before Iblis.

But Satan did not save him.

Look at the stages.

He began as a devout man who had withdrawn from society to worship. A woman was brought to him so that he could pray for God to heal her.

Then he committed a sexual sin.

Then he murdered an innocent person.

Then he bowed before Iblis.

And the whole descent began with one forbidden private encounter that he allowed himself to enter.

Unfortunately, private encounters of this kind have become widespread in our societies today because believers have become strangely casual about the matter.

## Our Capabilities in the Struggle Against Iblis

We ended the previous chapter with a question.

God created us and made us responsible for facing Iblis. So what abilities and resources has He given us for that struggle?

It is now time to answer that question. After that, we will look at a number of practical guidelines for confronting Iblis and defeating him.

Among the resources God has given us are the following:

1. **Reason:** God gave us minds so that we can think, understand, make sense of things, judge consequences and risks, recognize dangers and opportunities, and uncover methods, traps, deceptions, tricks, and schemes.

We can use all of this in our struggle against Iblis.

A person’s mind is one of the true measures of their worth. Someone who refuses to use their reason is little better off than someone who has none.

What use is a mind if we do not use it?

2. **Divine guidance:** From the very beginning of this struggle, God warned Adam and his wife that Iblis was their enemy and told them what he wanted from them.

Then He warned their descendants through the prophets, messengers, and revealed scriptures.

They told us about Iblis and about the part of the self that urges us toward evil. They showed us the path of guidance and the path of error. They taught us about purifying and disciplining ourselves, and warned us about the dangers of temptation and moral decline.

They also pointed out the weaknesses Iblis exploits so that we can protect ourselves and remain alert to his tricks.

They explained all of these things to us.

So reason is not the only thing we have been given. The prophets and messengers have taught humanity throughout history, and the book of divine guidance is still in our hands today.

3. **The accumulated experience of earlier generations:** Iblis’s long life is certainly one of his advantages. Our individual lives are short by comparison.

But humanity has something of its own: the accumulated experience of generation after generation.

When Imam Ali advised his son Hasan, he encouraged him to study the experiences of humanity as a whole and benefit from them as though he himself had lived through the lives of all those who came before him.

This is why the awareness of a person living today is not the same as that of someone living a hundred or a thousand years ago.

The simplicity of earlier times has given way to a much greater body of knowledge and experience. We have inherited a higher level of understanding, awareness, and ability to make sense of the world.

That is the result of accumulated human experience.

4. **Freedom of choice:** God created human beings free to choose. Iblis has no absolute power over us, nor does anyone else.

As we said in the previous chapter, God did not give Iblis some supernatural power that allows him to control us against our will.

It is therefore unacceptable to say that we are powerless to escape him.

We are free to choose whether we obey God and His prophets or obey Iblis.

5. **Will and determination:** God has given human beings the will, determination, and ability to resist.

These are among our most important strengths in the struggle against Iblis, provided that our will remains guided by reason.

If our determination weakens, or if desire and the lower self take control of it, it can lead us to ruin.

We saw the effects of this weakness among some Lebanese when Israel invaded Lebanon. We explained at the time that Israel was an enemy of all Lebanon and that its army was an occupying force. Some people accepted that argument intellectually.

But they still lacked the strength, will, and determination to confront it.

And because they lacked those things, they tried to explain away their defeat with excuses and arguments that simply did not hold up.

6. **The ability to endure and be patient:** God has also given every one of us a capacity for patience and endurance.

How much of that capacity we use depends on the choice we make.

A person can endure hunger, thirst, fighting, hard work, war, separation from home, and other hardships when circumstances require it.

We can see this clearly in the lives of people before modern technology transformed travel and communication.

In earlier times, travelling abroad to earn a living could mean being completely cut off from parents, wife, and children for years at a time.

Without the human capacity for endurance, no man could have spent years away from his family, and no family could have endured his absence.

This capacity God placed within us is therefore one of our central strengths in the struggle.

Without it, we could not face Iblis at all.

There is no struggle without patience, and there is no victory without patience.

This is especially true in our struggle against Iblis. We need patience to resist sin. We need patience to resist desires and pleasures when they lead us in the wrong direction.

In religious thought, this is regarded as one of the highest forms of patience.

These, then, are some of the most important resources God has given us. They are not the only ones, but they are enough for our purposes here:

**reason, divine guidance, accumulated human experience, freedom of choice, will and determination, and the capacity for patience and endurance.**

Now that we understand both Iblis’s abilities and our own, the next question is obvious:

What is our plan for confronting him?

How do we face Iblis and make sure that we defeat him rather than allowing him to defeat us?

How do we drive him to despair and expel him from within ourselves, just as God expelled him from Paradise?

That is what we will turn to next.

## Essential Guidance for Confronting Iblis

What follows is a set of guidelines drawn from divine and prophetic teachings, together with what human experience has taught us.

**1. Stay awake and alert.**

Let me explain with a military example.

Suppose we live in a village or military camp that an enemy wants to attack. If everyone is asleep, the enemy can simply enter and occupy the place without even having to fight. We will have lost from the very first hour.

So the first condition for success is to stay awake, alert, and attentive.

As I have said before, this struggle between us and Iblis continues around the clock, with every moment and every breath. We cannot afford to become careless.

**2. Keep watch over yourself.**

Now suppose everyone in the village is awake, but we are all sitting together at the village headman’s house, drinking tea.

The enemy can still enter and occupy the village. What good did staying awake do us?

Being awake only helps if guards are posted around the village and constantly watch what is happening around its borders. This is the alertness of a sentry.

The same principle applies within ourselves.

Each of us has to keep watch over ourselves. We cannot simply let our eyes look wherever they please, our ears listen to whatever they please, our tongues say whatever they please, our hands do whatever they please, and our feet carry us wherever they please.

They all need to be watched and kept within limits. We have to measure what we say, what we look at, and what we do against clear standards.

If our abilities, our bodies, and our desires are all left completely unguarded, there is no telling where they may take us.

**3. Cleanse the soul.**

Think of a shopkeeper who wants to keep insects out of his shop. The first thing he does is keep the place clean, because insects gather where there is dirt.

That is a good picture of what I mean.

If you want to keep Iblis and his forces from entering your heart, you have to cleanse it of the things that attract them. Iblis and his forces do not find the same foothold in a heart that has been purified of its vices.

This is really a preventive measure. It makes the struggle easier.

Instead of fighting Iblis in a place full of things he can use against us, we clean the place first. We remove whatever draws him toward us.

To purify the soul means working to free it from things like envy, uncontrolled anger, and the other weaknesses Iblis exploits, while developing the opposite virtues.

So I work, as much as I can, to reduce my envy and remain calm when I become angry.

Anyone who wants to go deeper into this subject can turn to books on ethics and spiritual development. They contain many practical programs for giving up bad qualities and developing good ones.

An angry person is not doomed to remain angry. Change is possible, provided the person follows a form of inner training based on the ethical teachings handed down from the Prophet’s family.

Through discipline, education, effort, and a steady spiritual program, an envious person can become warm and generous toward others. A miser can become giving. An ignorant person can become wise. A mean person can become noble. A coward can become courageous.

But this happens gradually.

If a person is terrified of heights, you cannot ask him immediately to jump from a height of eleven storeys. But you may be able to persuade him to begin at a height of a metre and a half, then one storey, then two, until little by little the barrier of fear is broken.

The same applies to someone who is afraid of battle. You do not take him straight to the front, where artillery and aircraft are striking. First you teach him how to fire a weapon. Then you train him. You gradually help him overcome his fear. Only then do you send him to the front.

The human soul is not incapable of change.

With effort and discipline, it can be purified. And as I have said, this is one of the most important ways of weakening Iblis, because in a purified soul he finds far less that he can exploit.

**4. Create an environment that keeps Iblis away.**

This follows naturally from the previous point.

Once we have removed from ourselves the things that give Iblis an advantage, we should also create conditions that drive him away when he tries to approach.

For example, avoiding private seclusion with a woman who is not one’s wife or close relative removes one of the opportunities Iblis can exploit. The same principle applies to anything else that draws us toward temptation.

One part of creating the right environment is choosing a good spouse.

A righteous wife can help a man remain steady and protected in the face of temptation. A person should therefore not be dazzled by beauty or wealth while ignoring character, goodness, purity, and integrity. That can lead to disaster.

The same applies to women: they should look for a righteous husband.

Good friends matter as well.

Bad company pulls you toward Iblis. Good people pull you toward God. They help you, strengthen you, and support you in your faith, in your struggle with yourself, and in your struggle against your own devil.

**5. Do good.**

The traditions repeatedly tell us to do good deeds, and they also show us which deeds are especially important. The prophets are God’s messengers to us, so in this struggle against Iblis we should follow their guidance.

Think again of a military battle.

We follow the instructions of generals and officers because they know which weapon suits which situation. For one target they may choose artillery, for another a machine gun, an explosive charge, an anti-armour weapon, direct fire, indirect fire, infiltration, or an open assault.

The struggle of the soul against Iblis, the devils, and the part of ourselves that urges us toward wrong is similar in this respect.

We have to turn to the One who created the human soul, because He knows its secrets and all its hidden corners.

The prophets and the infallible Imams are His messengers to us. The teachings handed down from them are divine guidance, and many of those teachings tell us which good deeds drive Iblis away.

Iblis and his forces flee from acts of obedience and worship.

First among these is prayer.

One of the hardest things for them to see is a person truly praying, because prayer is an act of obedience to God, and because it restrains a person from indecency and wrongdoing.

Against the whispering and deception of Iblis, prayer is like an antibiotic.

But I do not mean prayer performed mechanically. I mean prayer with attention, humility, and a heart that is present before God. That kind of prayer drives Iblis away, blocks his path, and eventually leaves him in despair.

The same is true of other acts of obedience.

There is a clear example of this in a narration from Imam al-Sadiq, who relates that the Messenger of God said to his companions:

> “Shall I tell you something which, if you do it, will drive Satan as far from you as east is from west?”

They said, “Yes.”

He said:

> “Fasting darkens his face. Charity breaks his back. Loving for God’s sake and helping one another in good deeds cut him off at the root. And seeking forgiveness cuts his very lifeline.” [^part07-p010-n1]

One of Iblis’s greatest aims is to lead a person into sin and then stop that person from asking forgiveness.

He constantly tries to stand between us and repentance, because God accepts the repentance of His servants.

So acts of obedience—above all, prayer offered with attention, humility, and genuine focus on God—drive Iblis and his forces away. They protect you, strengthen you, and make you steady.

**6. Finally, seek God’s help.**

God says:

> “Seek refuge in God from Satan, the accursed.” [^part07-p010-n2]

To seek refuge in God means turning to Him from beginning to end.

And I want to emphasize this point.

Whatever steps we take, whatever abilities we possess, and whatever spiritual programs we follow, the foundation of victory is still to ask God for help, seek refuge in Him, turn to Him, trust Him, and rely on Him.

That is why God says:

> “He has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord.” [^part07-p011-n1]

When we turn to God, ask for His help, and rely on Him, we can defeat not only Iblis but every devil.

God is the Strong, the Mighty, the Great, the One capable of all things, the Almighty Lord of the heavens and the earth.

And what are you asking Him to help you against?

A weak creature whose power is essentially a voice that misleads, beautifies falsehood, and deceives.

With God’s help, Iblis can be defeated.

So turning to God and seeking His help is an essential part of this struggle.

We therefore have real abilities in this battle. We know the path, and we have been given guidance.

What is required of us is to remain firm and patient.

We must not listen to Satan’s whispers. We must not open our hearts to him and let him beautify things for us, lie to us, deceive us, fill us with empty hopes, and persuade us to keep putting things off.

And however many sins we may have committed in this life, the door is still open.

We can still repent.

We can still ask forgiveness.

We can still return to God, do good, purify ourselves, and become clean again.

But we must not listen when Iblis tells us to wait.

None of us knows whether we will still be alive an hour or two from now.

That means we have to fight this battle consciously: awake, alert, watchful, disciplined, purified, doing good, and asking God for help.

If we do that, we can reach a point of firmness, strength, and protection. We can defeat Iblis and drive him out of our inner world.

This is possible. It is within our reach.

This is what the Messenger of God called the **greater jihad**.

A well-known narration reports that when some of his companions returned from a military expedition, he said to them, in effect:

> “Welcome to those who have completed the lesser jihad. The greater jihad still remains.”

He described armed struggle—facing swords, with people killed and wounded—as the **lesser jihad**.

The struggle against the self, and this struggle against Iblis, he called the **greater jihad**.

One reason may be that this battle is harder.

A person may have fought Israel, then fought the takfiri groups, and defeated them both. Yet that same person may collapse in the struggle against his own desires, against the part of the self that urges him toward wrong, and against the deceptions of Iblis and his devils.

History is full of people like this.

And if we return now to Karbala, this was the whole story.

The people involved in Karbala knew where the truth stood.

They knew that Husayn was in the right.

They knew who Husayn was, and they knew who Yazid was. They understood what Husayn represented for the Muslim community and what he meant to the Messenger of God.

The leaders knew it. The soldiers knew it.

This is why the story of Umar ibn Sa’d is so important. It is read every year, and it should continue to be read and repeated, because there is a profound lesson in it.

Or he could refuse to kill him and give up the position he had dreamed about his whole life.

This struggle occupied his mind throughout the night before he gave his answer.

But in reality, the argument was not simply between Umar and himself.

It was between Umar, his desires, and Iblis.

After all the back and forth, Iblis won.

So Umar ibn Sa’d’s problem was not lack of knowledge.

It was not that he lacked awareness or intelligence. He had not misunderstood the situation. He was not socially ignorant. He did not lack religious understanding. Nor had he simply misjudged the consequences.

What, then, happened to him?

Satan came to a man who craved power.

Umar wanted authority so badly that he was prepared to pay almost any price for it—even killing Husayn, the grandson of the Messenger of God, even entering Hell, even bringing upon himself the curse of God, the prophets, and humanity until the Day of Resurrection.

His love of power blinded his heart, his mind, his will, and his resolve.

That was the opening Iblis used.

He failed the test of loyalty to God.

And the opportunity to turn back remained open until the very last moment.

Even after al-Abbas had been killed, and before Husayn himself was killed, Umar could still have crossed over to Husayn’s side and saved his life to come.

## Our Central Struggle

For every one of us, then, the central issue should be the real struggle we are fighting against Iblis.

Every other battle is ultimately shaped by what happens in this one.

# Chapter Four

## Enjoining Good and Forbidding Evil in Imam Husayn’s Movement

## Introduction

In the previous chapter, we completed our discussion of the nature of the struggle between the human being and Iblis.

We began that discussion with a question about the deeper meaning of the struggle between Husayn and Yazid.

In this chapter, I want to take up another subject drawn directly from the words of Imam Husayn, one closely connected with everything we have already discussed.

When we look at Husayn’s own statements about Karbala, one of the most important is a passage we hear again and again. In it, he explains why he chose to take this stand:

> “I did not rise out of arrogance or vanity, nor to spread corruption or commit injustice. I rose only to seek reform in the community of my grandfather. I want to enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong, and to follow the way of my grandfather and my father, Ali ibn Abi Talib. Whoever accepts me by accepting the truth—God has the greater claim to truth. And whoever rejects me, I will remain patient until God judges between me and these people in truth. He is the best of judges.”

This tells us that one of the central principles behind Husayn’s movement in 60–61 AH was **enjoining good and forbidding wrong**.

That will be the subject of this chapter.

We will approach it in two parts.

First, we will look at the idea itself: what it means and what we need to understand about it.

Then we will move from theory to practice and ask how this principle applies in real life and what responsibilities it places on all of us.

## First: Understanding the Principle of Enjoining Good and Forbidding Evil

I will discuss this through six points.

**First: What do “good” and “wrong” mean?**

If we want to approach the subject clearly, we first have to define the two basic terms.

In the Islamic understanding, **good** means anything God approves of or commands in our actions, speech, and character.

Examples include telling the truth, returning what has been entrusted to us, helping the poor, reconciling people, teaching those who do not know, bringing people together, maintaining family ties, fulfilling people’s rights, and much more.

**Wrong**, by contrast, means anything God condemns and forbids in our actions, speech, and character.

Examples include lying, backbiting, spreading gossip, cursing and insulting people, stirring up conflict, killing an innocent person, violating people’s honor, stealing, mistreating one’s parents, harming one’s neighbors, breaking family ties, and many other things.

So, as you can see, in the Islamic understanding the standard for deciding what is good and what is wrong is God’s will, as expressed through the teachings of the religious law set out in the Qur’an and in the teachings of the Prophet’s family.

There is another point worth stressing here.

Everything God has commanded and called good, from the beginning of creation until the end of time, is in reality good for human beings in this life and the next.

And everything God has forbidden and called wrong, from the beginning of creation until the end of time, is in reality harmful and corrupting for human beings in this life and the next.

God Himself has no need of us.

Our worship does not benefit Him.

Neither do our struggles, our charity, our almsgiving, or anything else we do that is good.

And our sins do not harm Him.

Neither do our acts of aggression, our wrongdoing, or anything else we do that is evil.

So do not imagine that God commands good and forbids wrong because He gains something from our obedience.

He is the Self-Sufficient, the Great, the All-Powerful, the Holy.

Our goodness adds nothing to Him, and our evil takes nothing away from Him.

**Second:** By making the duty of enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong binding on every believing man and woman, and by giving it such a central place in the life of anyone committed to Islam, God is affirming a basic principle: **Islam gives people responsibilities toward others, not only responsibilities toward themselves.**

A person cannot simply fulfill their own duties, avoid what is forbidden, and then assume that this is enough because they have secured their own well-being and salvation. Religious duty also requires us to help guide other members of society toward what will bring them well-being in this life and the next.

We see the same principle throughout Islamic teaching: helping the oppressed, giving to those in need, coming to the aid of someone in distress, caring for orphans, and all the other forms of mutual support that religion strongly encourages. They all show that Islam asks a Muslim to care about more than personal concerns. We are also expected to care about our families, our communities, our surroundings, our country, and sometimes humanity as a whole.

This responsibility toward others applies in social, cultural, security, political, and economic matters. It also applies to morality. That is why Islam calls on people to enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong throughout the different parts of society.

So the second point is this: **enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong is a responsibility toward society as a whole.**

**Third:** Not everything that is right has the same degree of importance. Some things matter more than others because God’s will places greater importance on them. This is obvious enough. The examples we have mentioned do not all belong on the same level.

The same is true of wrongdoing. Not every wrong is equally serious. Some forms of corruption are worse than others, just as some good actions are more important than others.

For example, honoring a guest and saving a drowning person are both good actions. But clearly they are not equally important. Likewise, theft and murder are both wrong, but murder is plainly a far more serious wrong than theft.

Anyone acting in this field must therefore understand that good actions have an order of importance, from important to more important, and that wrongs have an order of seriousness, from bad to worse. That is the third point.

This principle appears very clearly in Islamic law. The available religious evidence shows that the greatest level of caution is required when human life is at stake. After that comes people’s honor and dignity, and then their property. Matters involving blood—in other words, the taking of a protected human life—receive the highest degree of protection. Matters involving honor come next, then matters involving property.

So this distinction between what is important and what is more important is built directly into Islamic law.

**Fourth:** As jurists explain in their books, the duty to enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong has conditions attached to it.

It is a religious obligation, but it only becomes binding when certain conditions are met. Many religious duties work in the same way. Fasting during Ramadan, for example, is obligatory, but a sick person who would be harmed by fasting is not required to fast at that time. They make up the fast later, subject to the relevant rules.

The same applies to the pilgrimage. Hajj is obligatory only for someone who has the means to perform it, and that ability includes financial means, personal safety, and other requirements.

So these are duties whose obligation depends on certain conditions.

Enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong works in the same way. Both are duties, but both have conditions set out in the books of Islamic law.

**Fifth:** Although enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong are obligatory, they are **collective duties**.

A collective duty is not something every individual must personally carry out. If one person, or a sufficient number of believers, fulfills it, the obligation falls from the rest. But if everyone abandons it, then everyone bears responsibility and will be called to account for failing to perform a duty that rested on the community as a whole.

Resistance offers an example. If an enemy attacks, defending the land, people’s lives, their honor, their property, and their dignity becomes a collective duty. If a sufficient group takes up that duty and succeeds in repelling the enemy, the obligation falls from everyone else. But if that group is not enough, then the rest remain responsible.

That is what a collective duty means.

**Sixth:** This is the most important point, and it brings us into the second part of our discussion.

As a general rule, in every time and place, in every society and every environment, we are required to enjoin **every** form of what is right. It is not the case that we must encourage one good thing but have no duty toward another.

The same is true of wrongdoing. Every wrong, in principle, is something that should be opposed.

But sometimes the necessary conditions are present while we simply do not have the ability to address every good and every wrong at once. A person may be able to promote some forms of good but not all of them, or oppose some wrongs but not all.

When that happens, we have to apply the principle we discussed earlier: **the more important must take priority over the important, and the more destructive wrong must take priority over the lesser wrong.**

This point requires great care. Poor judgment can lead some of us to spend our energy opposing small wrongs that have only a limited effect on our society while ignoring far more dangerous forms of corruption that threaten our very existence, our security, our lives, and the lives of other people.

So when we cannot address every good and every wrong, we must begin with what matters most and with what is most destructive.

We already gave the example of saving a drowning person and honoring a guest.

Suppose someone is drowning in a river. You cannot leave them to die because you are busy honoring a guest. Even if we assume that honoring a guest is obligatory, doing so in that situation would itself become wrong, because you would be neglecting a much greater and more urgent duty for the sake of a lesser one.

Sometimes there can even be a conflict between avoiding something normally forbidden and carrying out a much more important good action.

Suppose a person is drowning in a pond, but the only way to reach them is by crossing land that belongs to someone who does not allow anyone onto it without permission. Normally, using another person’s property without their consent is forbidden.

But in this case, Islamic law does not allow you to stand there waiting until you obtain permission from the landowner. Saving a human life is far more important than the normally forbidden act of crossing that land without permission.

That is the religious ruling.

With this, we have completed our discussion of the main legal and theoretical principles governing enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong.

I wanted to set these points out first because some of them are not clear to everyone. Some people may imagine, for example, that our religious duty requires us to confront every wrong in our society all at once: the small and the large, the personal and the social, the economic, cultural, political, security-related, and so on.

That would be true if we had the ability to do it.

But when we do not, we have to distinguish between what is important and what is more important. In today’s language, we would call this **setting priorities**.

It means that we must not occupy ourselves with something important while abandoning something more important, and we must not fight one form of corruption while leaving a far worse one untouched.

## Second: Putting the Principle into Practice

Let us now turn from the theory to its practical application.

We will do this by looking at a number of historical and contemporary examples. The first is the movement of Imam Husayn, who said—as we quoted in the introduction—that he had risen in order to enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong.

When Husayn left Medina in the year 60 AH, moral corruption was widespread there. The same was true in Mecca. Historical sources even describe the governor of Medina himself as a corrupt man.

This situation had come about through policies, deliberate decisions, and other developments that I will not go into here.

Yet despite all of this, Husayn turned his attention to what was **more important**. He confronted what he saw as the greatest and most dangerous form of corruption of his time: Yazid himself, with his character, his goals, and his project.

Husayn saw Yazid’s rule, his project and ambitions, and the prospect of giving him control over the Muslim community as the greatest corruption of all.

So he rose to confront it.

He began by refusing to pledge allegiance. Husayn regarded giving allegiance to Yazid as one of the gravest wrongs of that time. He refused it and called on others to refuse it because of the enormous danger he believed Yazid’s rule posed to Islam and the Muslim community.

This becomes very clear in the exchange reported between Husayn and the governor of Medina when the governor demanded his allegiance to Yazid.

Among Husayn’s words were:

> “Islam is finished if the community is afflicted with a ruler like Yazid.”

There is something very important in that sentence.

He did not say that prayer would be finished. He did not say fasting would be finished, or pilgrimage, or this good deed or that one, or the duty to forbid a particular wrong.

He said **Islam** would be finished—with all its values, meanings, principles, and laws.

So Husayn went after what he believed was the greater and more dangerous evil.

## A Look at Our Own Time

We can apply the same principle to events in our own recent history.

Take the experience of the resistance in Lebanon in 1982.

Israel invaded Lebanon and occupied nearly half the country. It could potentially have taken the other half as well. It had a whole set of objectives, which we have spoken about many times: objectives concerning the Palestinian resistance and the Palestinian cause, Lebanon and its future, Lebanon’s political system, control over Lebanese political decisions, peace agreements with Lebanon, control over the country, and so on.

Young believers today may have a duty to direct some of their energy toward enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong in worship, ethics, and personal conduct: encouraging people to attend mosques, reconciling people who have fallen out with one another, opposing gambling, drug use, sexual immorality, and other wrongs.

These remain responsibilities today.

But in 1982, our judgment was that the most dangerous wrong that had to be confronted was the occupation itself.

Israel’s control over our land and resources, our sovereignty, our lives, our wealth, and our dignity—its ability to shed our blood, seize our property, humiliate our people, and imprison our men and women—was, in our view, the greatest wrong we faced.

That was where the main priority had to lie.

This is why we spoke then, and still speak today, about the **priority of resistance**.

We know there are many forms of corruption in our country that should be confronted. But if we tried to confront every problem and every wrong at once, we would find ourselves in conflict with almost everyone, and in the end we would solve none of them.

So we have to set priorities and act according to them.

Another of the greatest wrongs in our region, in our view, has been the spread of American domination over many decades: domination over our countries, our political decisions, our political systems, our oil and other resources, and the transformation of our markets into consumers of American weapons and goods.

A clear example is the huge amount spent by the Saudi authorities in recent years, during their war in Yemen, buying American arms and ammunition worth tens and even hundreds of billions of dollars.

There is also American security influence across the region, which we believe has helped fill our countries with conflicts and wars through political interference and competing alliances.

This, too, is among the greatest wrongs that must be confronted.

Before moving on to some more specific issues, I want to recall the words of Imam Musa al-Sadr—may God return him and his two companions safely—who said at the time:

**Israel is absolute evil.**

The same remains true today. If we were looking for what we regard as the clearest embodiment of evil and wrongdoing, it would be Israel.

Imam Khomeini, may God be pleased with him, used to call Israel a **cancerous tumor**.

Israel, in this view, is the source of corruption. If a person truly wants to fight corruption, this is where they must begin. Someone who fights corruption here and there while abandoning the confrontation with Israel is fighting the children while leaving their mother untouched, because Israel is the mother of that corruption.

Behind Israel stands America, which we call the **Great Satan**.

One of the strange things about our time is that America presents itself as fighting terrorism, while anyone who looks carefully can see, in our view, that it is among terrorism’s greatest supporters: nurturing it, giving it strength, protecting it in one way or another, and using it to kill people, destroy armies, and tear societies apart.

So today we need to identify very carefully the gravest forms of wrongdoing and corruption on this earth and confront them.

At the top of that list, in our judgment, are American policies and the Zionist project.

Anyone who does not see these priorities can end up absorbed in a distorted version of enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong.

Consider Saudi Arabia, for example. It has had committees devoted to promoting virtue and preventing vice. Yet their attention was confined to things such as women driving cars, for which women were punished, or shopkeepers failing to close their businesses at prayer time.

Meanwhile, the country’s wealth, oil, and political decisions were, in our view, placed at the disposal of American power.

What kind of wrongdoing is someone opposing when their political decisions, armed forces, and military establishment are all at the command of what they call the Great Satan?

Can that really be called enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong?

No. It is a distortion of the principle and a falsification of what it actually means.

The conclusion I want to establish is simple:

**We have to set priorities.**

## Internal Dangers Facing Lebanese Society

I now want to turn to what I believe are some of the most urgent priorities for enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong.

Since 1982, we in the resistance, together with other factions, have fought to liberate our land from an occupation that we regarded as the greatest wrong.

To this day we remain in our front-line villages near the border, alongside the Lebanese army and the official security services, facing any Israeli aggression, threat, or infiltration.

On the other side of the country, along the eastern border in the Bekaa, we also fought the threat from takfiri terrorist groups, and we continue to confront that danger.

All of this was done to protect our families, our people, our country, and our dignity. Thousands of martyrs and thousands of wounded were sacrificed along the way.

These are duties upon us, and they are among the clearest examples of enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong.

But there is another side to the picture.

While the southern and eastern borders are being protected against aggression and terrorism, and while official agencies and ordinary people work around the clock to prevent terrorist attacks, destructive dangers are spreading **inside** our society.

I am not talking here about one particular community.

I mean the Lebanese people as a whole, and indeed everyone living on Lebanese soil: Lebanese citizens, Palestinian refugees, displaced Syrians, and foreign residents.

All the people living in Lebanon face internal dangers no less serious, in their own way, than the dangers at the southern and eastern borders.

There are many destructive forces surrounding our people that we need to confront as far as we are able.

One of them is **drugs**.

Lebanese people are absorbed in political and economic concerns, while another major threat has been spreading through the country. Everyone knows it exists, but it has not received the attention it deserves. If we continue to neglect it, we may one day wake up to a disaster.

I mean the problem of drugs.

There are two parts to this:

1. The trade and distribution of drugs.
2. Drug use itself.

Let me begin with something basic.

There is no serious religious, moral, or rational disagreement over the danger of drugs. This is not simply a question of what is religiously permitted or forbidden.

Islam, Christianity, Judaism, Buddhism, Hinduism, and people with no religion at all can agree that drugs pose a grave danger.

More precisely, there is a broad human consensus that this is a destructive problem that societies have to confront.

That is why countries everywhere have laws against drug trafficking and other parts of the drug trade. Governments have created major law-enforcement agencies devoted specifically to fighting the cultivation, production, distribution, and smuggling of drugs.

The agreement of religions and ordinary human reason on the seriousness of this problem means that we do not need a lengthy religious or philosophical argument to prove it.

What we need is to bring the problem into the open.

We have some statistics on the number of addicts and drug users in Lebanon.

It is no exaggeration to say that the proportion of addicts among the people living in Lebanon is extremely high. The same is true if we include users who have not yet reached the point of addiction.

The figures point to a huge problem and a frightening danger spreading through the country.

State institutions and the relevant security agencies are making serious efforts, and they deserve credit for them. They seize smuggled shipments, arrest major traffickers, and detain some users and addicts.

But none of this changes what the statistics tell us.

The numbers remain alarming.

Worse still, we see no sign that the situation is improving. The figures continue to rise. Things are getting worse, particularly among young people between the ages of eighteen and thirty.

There may be a straightforward explanation for part of this. Some people see an opportunity to make enormous profits by exploiting demand for drugs, and so they enter the trade.

That certainly happens.

But there is another possibility that we should not simply dismiss: hostile security or intelligence agencies may also have an interest in spreading drugs inside particular communities.

Take the Palestinian camps.

If you wanted to destroy a camp—and thereby damage part of the Palestinian cause—one of the most effective methods would be to destroy its younger generation by flooding it with drugs.

Why should we simply rule out the possibility that Mossad could be involved in something like that?

The same argument applies to our own communities.

We know that our enemy will not rule out any method that might weaken our society. And from the enemy’s point of view, drugs have an obvious advantage: there is no need to begin a military war.

The enemy avoids the risks that come with opening a military front—the loss of soldiers, the political and psychological cost, and attacks on its own cities.

Instead, this method can eat away quietly at the heart of society and destroy the entire rear line: our families, our young men, and our young women, who are the real foundation of the country, its army, its resistance, its institutions, its security, and its economy.

This is precisely where the greatest danger lies today.

As I said, young people make up the largest group being targeted or becoming involved.

Even more disturbing is that, within that age group, students appear to have the highest rate of use: university students, secondary-school students, and even younger pupils.

Different kinds of drugs—pills, cannabis, and others—are being offered in different forms and colors at very low prices.

That, at the very least, strengthens suspicions that there may be organized efforts behind their spread.

No one should pretend that this is simply a matter of personal freedom.

A person cannot say, “I am free to take drugs,” or, “I am free to sell them.”

No serious legal system recognizes such a freedom. Religion does not. The law does not. Reason does not.

All of them recognize the enormous harm drugs cause both to the individual and to the public as a whole.

At the personal level, the user destroys themselves. Addiction damages the mind, the emotions, and the person’s ability to live a normal human life.

We all know what serious addiction means and where it can lead. There is no need for me to go into all of that here.

At the social level, its effects can be devastating.

Drug addiction destroys families. Many divorces today are connected to drugs, leaving families broken apart and children lost between them.

It also produces wider social harm. An addict may be driven toward other crimes: theft, murder, sexual wrongdoing, and more.

Addiction can become a gateway to many other forms of corruption.

The trafficking of girls that appeared in our society some time ago, for example, cannot be separated entirely from the drug problem.

So we are dealing with an extremely serious issue.

All of us need to raise our voices about it just as strongly as we raise our voices about other public demands, because this threat is even more dangerous in some respects.

Drugs have spread into every part of Lebanon.

The rate may differ from one place to another, but the danger exists everywhere. Even conservative towns and villages have been reached.

So drug trafficking and addiction have become some of the greatest wrongs we need to confront.

Doing so does not compete with the other responsibilities I mentioned earlier. It strengthens them.

If all we do is protect the borders while leaving our society open to destruction from within, then eventually there will be no society left for us to defend.

We cannot exaggerate the need for prevention.

Some countries impose the death penalty for selling, smuggling, or distributing drugs because they regard the damage as so severe.

If you asked me which is more dangerous to society—a takfiri extremist who plants a bomb and kills three or four innocent young people, or a criminal who enters a secondary school and turns our young people into drug addicts—I would say the second is more dangerous.

The first may cross our defenses and enter our community, but the danger can be contained by stronger security measures. And those he kills die as innocent martyrs.

The second is different.

His danger has no clear limit, and his victims risk losing both this life and the next.

## Our Responsibilities in Confronting This Danger

So what is our responsibility in the face of this wrongdoing?

Islamic law traditionally sets out three levels for opposing wrongdoing: **with the hand, with the tongue, and in the heart**.

Opposing it “with the hand” is the responsibility of the state. In Islamic law, this kind of enforcement belongs to the lawful authority. Without getting into legal and constitutional details, it is therefore more accurate to place this responsibility on the authorities running the country. No one else is being asked to take enforcement into their own hands. It is the state’s job to arrest people, conduct raids, imprison suspects, bring cases to court, prosecute offenders, and impose fines on anyone proven to be involved in this crime.

But that does not remove responsibility from the rest of us. The state is not the only party concerned. We too, as a people and as a resistance, have a duty to oppose this wrongdoing—in our hearts and with our words.

And I am not talking about something merely recommended. This is a religious duty. We are not free to neglect it. If we do, we will be questioned about it on the Day of Judgment.

To begin with, all of us need to raise our voices and demand that the state carry out its responsibilities fully.

And this cannot be reduced to security measures alone. A real solution requires a complete plan. It should begin with providing alternative crops—and no one should misunderstand me here: the lack of alternative crops is not a religious, legal, or moral excuse for growing narcotics. The plan must also address conditions in schools, public education and awareness through the media and the education system, the responsibilities of the courts, and finally the security dimension.

In other words, what is needed is a comprehensive, carefully designed plan that works on every level.

This cannot be postponed or endlessly delayed. That is the responsibility of the state.

As for our own direct responsibility as ordinary people, we have one responsibility toward drug dealers and traffickers, and another toward our young people and children who become involved in drug use.

Our first responsibility toward dealers and traffickers is to reject what they do **in our hearts**.

That means making it part of our understanding and our culture that producing, selling, and trafficking drugs are wrong; that they bring nothing but corruption and destruction; that they anger God; and that they are rejected by religion and reason alike.

That is what it means to reject wrongdoing in the heart.

This matters because if that inner rejection disappears, then over time society can begin to treat these crimes as normal. Drug dealing can become just another familiar business in the neighborhood, no more unusual than selling clothes or food.

The second responsibility is to oppose it **with our words**.

Every part of society has a role here: ordinary people, schoolteachers, university professors, religious scholars, political leaders, party officials, community figures, and others. We should confront the dealers among our own families, villages, towns, and neighborhoods and use every reasonable form of advice, warning, and pressure to make them stop.

We need to tell every dealer plainly:

What you are doing is killing us. It is setting our communities on fire. It is tearing apart the foundations of our society. What you are doing poses a greater danger to us than everything the Israeli enemy and the takfiri enemy are trying to do.

If someone is afraid to confront a powerful dealer directly, then let them turn to people who are not afraid of those with influence, authority, and power.

We are asking for nothing except what pleases God and protects our communities.

So are our community leaders actually doing this?

I do not have exact figures for how much effort is being made to confront dealers verbally, but I am certain of one thing: what is being done in our cities, villages, and neighborhoods is not enough.

As for me personally, I consider this a religious duty that falls on my own shoulders.

So I address every drug dealer and every trafficker in Lebanon.

First, I appeal to you on human, moral, religious, and national grounds.

Enough.

Stop killing and destroying our young people, our families, our villages, our cities, our security, our economy, and our moral life.

Fear God.

And if you have no religion, then at least be free human beings with moral principles. Hold on to the bare minimum of human feeling. Can you not see what your merchandise is doing to your own society and your own people?

When we speak out and oppose this wrongdoing with our words, we have to explain its consequences clearly. We have to make people understand what is happening, and there must be broad public condemnation of it.

But more important than speaking is getting a result.

The point is for these people to hear what we are saying and take it seriously. Some may listen to advice, come to their senses, ask God for forgiveness, and abandon what they are doing.

That is the real religious purpose of opposing wrongdoing.

When someone responds, we should work to find practical solutions, ways out, and reasonable arrangements that help them leave this activity behind.

But others will refuse to listen because of the money they make from the trade. In their case, the authorities must move to the level of enforcement.

There is another measure ordinary people can take as well: **social boycott**. This too is a responsibility that falls on the public, and I am calling for it clearly and strongly.

If someone is publicly known to be involved in this crime, people should boycott them.

Do not receive them warmly in your homes or workplaces. Do not go out of your way to visit them. Do not smile at them as though nothing is wrong. Do not sit with them socially. Do not take part in their weddings, celebrations, funerals, or other family occasions.

Even if they are our own brothers.

This kind of social boycott does not create civil conflict or physical confrontation. It costs nothing, and we do not need the state to carry it out. Every person is free to withdraw socially from people involved in this trade.

Unfortunately, the reality in our country today is very different.

Drug dealers have become a socially respected group. Some have even become prominent figures in their villages and districts. They give out money, provide assistance, and people turn to them to solve local social problems.

This is a disaster, and no one knows how far its consequences may eventually take us.

Some of our people may say: we need to remain open to every part of society because our priority is the resistance, and the resistance needs the support of all social groups.

My answer is this:

This group is an enemy of the resistance, an enemy of the society that supports the resistance, and an enemy of the resistance’s culture and values.

They betray the blood of the martyrs.

They betray the wounded who still live among us.

They betray our values and our moral principles.

They are destroying us, our surroundings, and our society.

Who said we are supposed to embrace them?

No. We should boycott them and isolate them socially.

If dealers are isolated within their own families, clans, villages, and towns, they will eventually be forced to reconsider what they are doing. Human beings cannot live entirely alone.

Let me give another example to make the point clearer: collaboration with Israel.

There were periods in Lebanon when collaboration with Israel was treated by some people almost as a matter of opinion or a political choice. But over time, because of changes in the law, the courts, public culture, the media, and politics, it came to be treated as a crime punishable by law.

The collaborator became an outcast in their own community and family. Their actions became a source of shame to their relatives.

But today, the families of drug dealers often feel no comparable shame, because society does not make them feel that what is happening is disgraceful.

There was an incident some time ago that was both absurd and tragic.

Security forces were uncovering groups of collaborators connected to foreign intelligence networks. In one case, they raided the home of a collaborator in Beirut’s southern suburbs and arrested him in full view of the public.

As they were taking him away, he shouted that he was a **drug dealer, not a collaborator**.

He was trying to clear his name in front of the people.

That tells us something important. In the general social consciousness, drug dealing is still not treated as one of the gravest offenses.

That is our responsibility toward dealers and traffickers.

As for the people who actually use drugs, the first responsibility falls on their families.

Fathers and mothers need to pay close attention to their children’s behavior. Drug use has become widespread and deeply present in many schools. Parents therefore have a responsibility alongside the Ministry of Education and the security services. Everyone has a part to play.

But the role of the family is central, both before addiction begins and after it has taken hold.

Before addiction, parents need to remain aware of their child’s behavior and make a serious effort to raise them with the moral and religious foundations they need.

After addiction, once the problem has been discovered, their responsibility is to help the child receive proper treatment.

Treatment for addiction is available in our country.

Parents must never surrender to the idea that their son or daughter is beyond hope. They are responsible for helping them recover, and so are the other members of the family and the wider community around them.

School principals and teachers also carry a major responsibility.

They must pay attention to the condition of their students, who have been entrusted to their care. There can be no carelessness here and no turning a blind eye to warning signs.

And just as families and schools have responsibilities, every active institution in the country has a duty to stand beside our children and young people who have fallen into addiction. Each must act within its own area of responsibility and take the necessary measures without delay or procrastination.

As I said before, this issue belongs among our priorities.

It does not compete with our other priorities. On the contrary, dealing with it strengthens and protects everything else we are trying to defend.

So I hope everyone will reconsider the seriousness of this danger, give it the attention it deserves, and use every available means to confront it.

But I want to go further than simply expressing hope.

This is a duty.

All of us have to act, without hesitation and without feeling embarrassed before any clan, family, organization, or other group.

We should be more ashamed before God, His Messenger, and the Imams.

We should be more ashamed before the martyrs and their families, before the wounded and everything they have suffered, and before the fighters who carry their lives in their hands.

This is about protecting our country, our society, our values, our homes, and our security.

In closing, one of the most important lessons we can learn from Imam Husayn’s movement, from the nights and days of Ashura, is the principle of **enjoining what is right and opposing what is wrong**.

And that principle opens the way to many other virtues: honesty, sincere advice, concern for other people and for one’s country, resisting corruption, putting others before oneself, sacrifice, patience, and much more.

We ask Almighty God to help us spread what is good in our country, and to confront this problem and others like it through cooperation between the state and the people.

That cooperation is one of the golden formulas through which we can deal with this problem properly and bring it to an end.

Lebanon must not become a home for drugs or a transit route for them, as some would like.

But preventing that depends on all of us accepting our responsibilities.

# Chapter Five

## Lessons from Husayn’s Movement: The Meaning and Practice of Patience

In the first chapter of this book, we discussed the theme we chose for Ashura this year: **“Patience and Victory.”** There we focused on the second part of that theme—**victory**. In this chapter, we will turn to the first: **patience**. In doing so, we will build on some of the points discussed in earlier chapters.

As we have already said, God created us and placed us on this earth with two lives before us: this life and the next.

Our life in this world is a period of testing. Each human being is tested as an individual, and humanity is tested as a whole. The Qur’an repeatedly describes this reality in terms of **trial** and **testing**. God says:

> “He is the One who created the heavens and the earth in six days, while His Throne was upon the water, so that He might test you as to which of you is best in deeds.” [^part10-p004-n1]

In other words, our existence in this world is surrounded by tests and trials. Each of us is being given the chance to show what kind of person we are and who among us acts best.

God also says:

> “Then We made you successors on the earth after them, so that We might see how you would act.” [^part10-p004-n2]

So our lives and actions matter. What we believe and what we do in this world will determine our fate in the next.

God has provided this earth with everything human beings need. He has placed its resources at our disposal and given us the freedom to choose our path—in belief, thought, culture, and conduct. He has given us reason. He has shown us the path of truth and made the path of falsehood clear as well:

> “And We showed him the two paths.” [^part10-p005-n1]

God has illuminated the way for us through this general guidance. He then sent prophets and messengers, revealed heavenly books, and gave us divine laws to guide every part of our lives.

As I have also said in earlier chapters, God clearly identified our main and true enemy: Iblis and his forces. He showed us our own strengths and weaknesses as well.

And after giving us all of this, He placed us in this world—the place where we must act—and, in effect, said: **Now show Me what you will do. Let us see who succeeds in the test of life.**

## Kinds and Levels of Divine Testing

The things through which God tests us can be grouped under several main headings.

**1. Acts of obedience.**

The first is obedience: the things God has commanded us to do, what Islamic law calls our religious duties. These include prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, zakat, khums, striving in God’s cause, enjoining what is right, and forbidding what is wrong.

The test is simple: **Will we do what has been required of us? Will we remain committed to these acts of obedience or not?**

**2. Sins and wrongdoing.**

The second is sin: lying, backbiting, spreading malicious talk, killing an innocent person, mistreating one’s parents, aggression, injustice, and other forms of wrongdoing.

Here the test is whether we avoid these things or give in to them.

**3. Hardship and misfortune.**

The third is hardship.

Ever since Adam, his wife, and Iblis came down to the earth, human life has been filled with misfortune. People can be struck by illness, poor health, difficult circumstances, financial loss, or harm to their children, loved ones, and families.

Here the question is: **How will we respond?**

Will hardship break us? Will it make us lose our way and abandon our devotion to God? Will it cause us to neglect our duties or commit sins?

Or will we endure and stand firm?

**4. Blessings.**

The fourth kind of test is **blessing**.

Everything God has given us in this world is itself a test. His blessings cannot even be counted:

> “If you tried to count God’s blessings, you could never number them.” [^part10-p007-n1]

Some blessings are shared by everyone; others are particular to individuals.

Here God tests us to see whether we will be grateful or ungrateful. Will we recognize these blessings and use them in obedience to God, for the good of other people, and for our own good in this world and the next? Or will we misuse them, turn them against our Lord, and use them in ways that harm others and ourselves?

All of these are forms of testing. And understanding that prepares us for the subject of patience.

There is something else we should remember.

This world is not Paradise, where we can simply eat freely from whatever we desire. From the moment a person is born, he or she has to work. This has been true of every human being, in every generation, in every time and place, since Adam first came to the earth.

People have to work, struggle, and make an effort to protect themselves, their health, and their families. They must secure food, water, safety, dignity, comfort, happiness, health, and peace of mind.

God has made all these things available in the world, but they do not simply arrive without effort. Human beings have to work for them. We have to face difficulties, suffering, and challenges, and we have to make sacrifices.

So here we are, in the middle of life, naturally striving to meet our basic needs while at the same time facing all the tests we have just described.

And the road is not easy.

An enemy lies in wait for us, trying to block our path and set traps through temptation and whispering. We also have to contend with our own lower self, the part of us that pulls toward wrongdoing, and with our own desires.

If we are going to continue on this road without losing our way, and if we are going to overcome its difficulties, challenges, and tests, then we need a number of things: faith, reason, God-consciousness, knowledge, planning, cooperation among believers, unity, organization, and persistent work.

But at the head of all of these—the foundation of our ability to face the struggle—is **patience**.

## What Patience Means and What It Brings in This Life and the Next

In His Book, God directs us toward patience and presents it as one of the first things we should rely on when confronting the difficulties of life:

> “Believers, seek help through patience and prayer. God is with those who are patient.” [^part10-p009-n1]

And elsewhere:

> “Seek help through patience and prayer. It is difficult except for the humble—those who know that they will meet their Lord and return to Him.” [^part10-p009-n2]

These verses make the point very clearly: **patience and prayer are two of the main supports we need on this road.**

Prayer deserves a discussion of its own, so we will leave it aside here. Our subject now is patience.

In Arabic, *sabr*—patience—literally carries the sense of holding oneself together when faced with panic, pain, misfortune, confrontation, or other forms of pressure. It means restraining and controlling oneself when under strain.

We could also describe it as endurance, self-discipline, the ability to absorb pressure and hardship, and the inner effort to overcome them.

But before going further, we need to clear up one important point.

When the Qur’an and the religious traditions speak so highly of patience—when they describe it as one of our main supports in life and a path to goodness in the next life—they do **not** mean passive acceptance of misery.

Patience does not mean that if we are oppressed, we simply accept oppression and submit to it. It does not mean that if we are hungry, we quietly endure hunger without trying to earn food. It does not mean that if our land is occupied, or our lives, dignity, and property are attacked, we remain silent and patiently accept the aggression.

That is not the kind of patience we are talking about.

Patience here means enduring hardship while continuing to resist. It means inner strength and spiritual steadiness in the face of every difficulty, whether small or great, that confronts us as individuals or as a community.

Once we understand this, we can see why the Qur’an speaks about patience so often. Forms of the Arabic root appear more than seventy times: *be patient*, *they were patient*, *those who are patient*, and so on.

Simply looking through the Qur’anic verses that use this word is enough to show how much importance the Qur’an gives to the subject.

The same is true of the traditions. Patience appears in a very large number of reports—far too many to quote here. They describe patience as being to faith what the head is to the body. They also present it as fundamental not only to religious life but to every part of life: politics, military affairs, security, society, earning a living, and much more.

In short, patience is one of the foundations of success in life.

Imam al-Sadiq is reported to have said:

> “Patience is to faith as the head is to the body. When the head is gone, the body is gone. In the same way, when patience is gone, faith is gone.” [^part10-p012-n1]

The meaning is powerful.

Faith cannot survive without patience. Without it, a person cannot remain consistent in obedience, cannot stand firm against forbidden desires and sin, and may collapse when misfortune strikes and lose his faith.

Imam Ali is also reported to have said:

> “Patience in all affairs is like the head to the body. When the head leaves the body, the body is ruined. And when patience leaves an affair, that affair is ruined.” [^part10-p012-n2]

This takes the idea beyond faith alone. Patience is necessary in **every part of life**.

We can see this around us every day.

A student who has no patience will not succeed in learning and will fail his examinations. A community that has no patience cannot live peacefully alongside other communities that differ from it, because living together requires people to tolerate one another.

And the same principle applies in countless other areas: without patience, things fall apart and whatever good could have come from them is lost.

In this sense, patience is what truly makes a person strong.

We are not speaking about patience only as a moral virtue that God asks us to develop so that we can receive a spiritual reward. We mean patience as **endurance, steadiness, and the inner strength that allows us to face the challenges of life without breaking**.

This is why psychologists and social thinkers emphasize patience as a basic tool in any serious struggle.

Intelligence matters. Resources matter. Skill and competence matter. But patience underlies them all.

Imagine an army with a strong position, experienced commanders, enormous military resources, and highly capable fighters. But suppose its soldiers cannot endure a long war. They cannot tolerate sacrifice. They are unwilling to suffer or give anything up. They simply do not have the patience required for hardship.

That army will be defeated.

Now imagine another side with less intelligent commanders, fewer military resources, and fighters who are not as highly trained—but whose soldiers are far more patient and far more able to endure.

Victory may very well belong to them.

The Qur’an points to this principle at both the individual and collective level:

> “Prophet, urge the believers to fight. If there are twenty among you who are steadfast, they will overcome two hundred. And if there are a hundred among you, they will overcome a thousand of those who disbelieve, because they are a people who do not understand.” [^part10-p015-n1]

Notice what the verse says.

It does not say, “If there are twenty fighters among you,” or twenty horsemen, or—to put it in modern terms—twenty tank drivers or twenty generals.

Those are separate details.

It says: **twenty who are steadfast.**

Patience is the quality that allows those twenty to overcome two hundred.

So patience gives strength. It steadies a person and allows them to remain effective. That is why the traditions speak about what patience brings us in this world even before the next.

Patience does more than help us survive what has already happened. It gives us fresh strength to **keep going**.

Again, this is not passive endurance. We can see the meaning in the example of al-Abbas. Even after losing his hands, he continued as long as he still had the ability to move forward, the possibility of bringing water, confronting the enemy, and defending Husayn.

He continued with patience, seeking God’s reward.

That is the patience we mean: the kind that gives us the energy, strength, and ability to continue working and moving forward. It keeps us from collapsing in the face of desire, fear, exhaustion, disaster, the loss of someone we love, hunger, or any other hardship.

The Prophet is reported to have said:

> “Victory comes with patience.” [^part10-p016-n1]

In other words, there is no victory without patience.

If we look at our own experience, we can say that the resistance in Lebanon won through patience: the patience of our people in the villages and cities, those who were displaced and whose homes were bombed, and those who lived for decades along the front lines.

It was the patience of the fighters, the families of the martyrs, the wounded and their families, the prisoners and detainees in the prisons and their families.

All of them endured enormous difficulties and challenges despite the security war, the military war, the destruction, the psychological warfare, intimidation, and constant efforts to break their morale.

The resistance and its fighters remained patient. The people around the resistance remained patient, supported it, and continued to endure.

That patience made these victories possible.

Patience is also the key to relief. Through it come success and triumph, peace of mind and contentment, and the ability to reach higher goals.

Someone seeking a certain level of knowledge needs patience. Someone trying to improve the welfare of a community and solve its problems needs patience. Someone defending their country, dignity, and sovereignty against powerful tyrants needs patience.

None of these efforts succeeds without it.

Patience also makes suffering easier to bear. It lightens anxiety and hardship. As one saying puts it, patience is **“the greatest help against the hardships of time.”**

Everything we have discussed so far concerns what patience gives us in this world.

As for the next life, many Qur’anic verses speak of the immense reward God has prepared for those who remain patient, and of the Paradise and happiness they receive because of their patience.

We will mention only one:

> “Say: My believing servants, be mindful of your Lord. Those who do good in this world will have good. God’s earth is vast. Those who are patient will be given their reward without measure.” [^part10-p018-n1]

This is an extraordinary promise.

Those who truly belong among the patient will not have a record opened for them, nor a scale set up, nor be subjected to ordinary reckoning. They will simply be told: **Enter the everlasting gardens and receive your full reward, without limit.**

A tradition from Imam al-Sadiq, transmitted from the Prophet, expresses this meaning.

The Prophet is reported to have said:

> “When the records are opened on the Day of Resurrection and the scales are set up…”

This is the great judgment in which every detail of a person’s life is laid bare, from the moment they became morally responsible until the moment the soul left the body. Nothing can be forged, denied, or hidden. The earth, the sky, the walls, your hands and feet, your skin, your tongue, and your eyes all bear witness.

According to the Prophet, what saves a person who has endured such trials is this:

> “No scale will be set up for those who were afflicted, and no record will be opened for them.”

Of course, this means those who endured affliction **with patience**. It does not mean people who collapsed under pressure and became collaborators, traitors, corrupt people, thieves, criminals, killers, drug dealers, or people who spread indecency and wrongdoing through their society.

The Prophet then recited:

> “Say: My believing servants, be mindful of your Lord. Those who do good in this world will have good. God’s earth is vast. Those who are patient will be given their reward without measure.” [^part10-p019-n1]

## Forms of Patience

The religious traditions divide patience into three main forms:

* patience in obedience,
* patience in resisting sin,
* and patience in hardship.

The meaning of each is fairly clear, but they deserve some explanation.

Take prayer.

When a person first reaches the age of religious responsibility, becoming consistent in prayer takes effort. Later, it may become a normal part of life that a person performs almost automatically. But at the beginning, praying five times every day—especially praying at the proper time, and above all getting up for the dawn prayer—requires patience.

The same is true of fasting, especially during long summer days.

Pilgrimage requires patience.

Striving in God’s cause requires patience.

Giving money where religious law requires it—paying khums or zakat, for example—requires effort and patience.

These are all examples of the first kind: **patience in obedience**.

Carrying out our duties to God requires a great deal of it.

The second kind is **patience in resisting sin**.

For some people, giving up certain sins is very difficult. They cannot do it without patience.

Someone who has become used to backbiting may find it extremely hard to stop. That requires patience.

Someone whose tongue has become accustomed to lying needs patience to stop lying.

Resisting forbidden desires may require even greater patience, especially for a person who does not have an easy lawful way to satisfy a natural desire while an unlawful path lies wide open before them.

Turning away from that path can be extremely difficult.

Patience is one of our strongest supports in staying away from sin.

The third kind is **patience in hardship**: enduring misfortune without allowing it to break us.

Every one of us knows what this means.

It means enduring the loss of people we love, the martyrdom of loved ones, the suffering of the wounded, poverty, hunger, harsh living conditions, and the many other forms of hardship that affect our country, our region, and indeed the whole world.

All of these require patience.

A person is rewarded for patience in each of these three areas when that patience is **for God**.

And here, reward depends on intention.

Suppose someone endures the hunger of fasting during Ramadan, but does so only because they want to lose weight.

They will have no reward for that fasting before God in the next life, because their goal was worldly and they already received the worldly result they wanted.

God has established laws in this world through which people receive the natural results of their actions regardless of their intention. That is another subject and not one we need to explore here.

The point is this:

If a person’s patience is for God, their obedience is for God, and they avoid sin for God’s sake—in response to His commands and prohibitions—then God will certainly record a reward for them.

In some hadiths, the best kind of patience is said to be **patience in resisting sin**. If we ask why, the answer may be that the forces pushing us toward sin can be extremely powerful. On one side there is Iblis, constantly trying to draw us toward wrongdoing. On the other are our own desires and appetites, which make us more receptive to his whispers, together with the self within us that urges us toward evil.

Resisting all of this requires real strength. A person has to stop themselves from crossing the line: from drinking what is forbidden, eating what is forbidden, or entering into a forbidden relationship.

Take anger, for example. Anger is one of the greatest dangers because it can lead a person into all kinds of sins. To restrain yourself when anger takes hold requires patience at its highest level. This helps us understand why some traditions say that the best form of patience is patience in resisting sin.

Other traditions, however, say that the greatest patience is **patience in the face of devastating loss**—the hardest and most painful kind of calamity.

These traditions can be brought together if we remember that not every act of obedience involves the same degree of difficulty. Prayer is easier than fasting. Fasting for the thirty days of Ramadan is easier than struggling for decades. For some people, fasting may even be easier than paying *khums*, for example. So acts of obedience differ in the burden they place on us.

The same is true of sin. Some sins are relatively easy to avoid. Others carry a powerful attraction. Desire pulls strongly toward them, and Satan makes them a central point of attack, mobilizing all his efforts to draw the person in. Resisting that kind of temptation takes much more effort.

Calamities also differ. A person injuring their hand is not the same as losing the hand entirely. Losing a hand is not the same as losing a child. And losing one child is not the same as losing two or three children at once.

So perhaps the best kind of patience is patience in the face of whatever is hardest to bear. That is why the tradition speaks of enduring **the bitterness of devastating loss**. When the suffering is severe, patience becomes correspondingly harder—and therefore greater. The reward is proportionate to the difficulty and to what the person has had to endure.

This is why we always say that the patience shown by Imam Husayn, his companions, and his sons in everything that happened at Karbala, and the patience shown by Zaynab and the women and children with her, was among the greatest and highest forms of patience imaginable. There is no real argument about this. It was patience in the face of the bitterest loss.

## How Do We Develop Patience?

Now that we have discussed what patience means and what it teaches us, an important question remains: **where do we get it from?** How do we develop this moral quality?

The first source of patience is the One who holds the treasures of the heavens and the earth: God Almighty.

We should turn to Him and ask Him to help us, to give us the patience we need in the trials we face. God is generous, giving, merciful, gentle, and compassionate. He does not withhold His gifts from His servants.

If someone is facing a calamity, struggling with a powerful desire, or finding it hard to summon the will to carry out an act of obedience, and that person turns to God and asks Him for patience, strength, firmness, and support, God will answer.

After all, it is God who tells us to seek help through patience and prayer. And it is God who promises us that **God is with the patient**. According to the commentators, the “being with” mentioned in this verse means being with them through help and support. God helps the patient. He strengthens them. He stands with them. He does not abandon them or leave them to themselves. He supports them, guides them, and gives them victory.

So the first thing is to ask God for patience. He has promised to be with us when we strive to remain patient.

This is why the Messenger of God is reported to have said:

> “Whoever seeks patience, God will grant them patience.”

In other words, whoever asks God for patience and asks Him for help will receive His help.

The second source is **the remembrance of God**.

This goes beyond simply praying for help. The very act of remembering God when we face personal or collective hardship gives us strength and helps us endure.

God says:

> “We will certainly test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth, lives, and crops. But give good news to those who are patient.” [^part11-p005-n1]

This is one of the laws of life in this world.

“Something of fear” means the loss of security, something many countries experience today. “Hunger” points to hardship in daily life and economic distress. “Loss of wealth and lives” includes losing the people we love.

Then God says: “Give good news to those who are patient.”

Who are they? What enables them to endure all this and receive that good news?

God says:

> “Those who, when calamity strikes them, say: ‘We belong to God, and to Him we return.’” [^part11-p005-n2]

That is itself an act of remembering God.

What is striking is that this has become deeply rooted in our religious and social culture. When someone in a religious community receives painful news—the death of someone they love, for example—they often say almost instinctively:

> “We belong to God, and to Him we return.”

We grew up hearing this from our parents and grandparents. Religion made it part of our everyday culture.

But the remembrance meant here is not mindless repetition. It is remembrance with awareness and understanding.

Think about the words:

> “We belong to God, and to Him we return.”

“We belong to God” means that our very existence belongs to Him. He gives life and takes life. He gives honor and allows humiliation. He raises and lowers. He tests us, forgives us, judges us, and gives us victory.

We belong to Him. Our children belong to Him. Our husbands and wives belong to Him. The wealth we possess and the fruits we enjoy are all in His hands. Our lives and our destinies depend on His will.

“And to Him we return” reminds us that after death our final return is to Him.

That gives us a different relationship with death and with the loss of people we love, because those we lose are returning to God.

Repeating these words—and others like **“There is no power and no strength except through God”**, or **“Whatever God wills; there is no power except through God”**—has a real psychological effect. It steadies a person and calms them.

God says:

> “Surely, in the remembrance of God hearts find peace.” [^part11-p007-n1]

And regarding those who remember God when disaster strikes, when hardship comes, or whenever patience is required, God says:

> “Those are the ones upon whom are blessings from their Lord and mercy, and those are the rightly guided.” [^part11-p007-n2]

Through this remembrance of God—the living awareness that God is present and sees us—and through a real understanding of what that remembrance means, God gives us three things.

1. **Blessings from their Lord.** Think of what it means for God Himself to bless a human being. Here, God’s blessing means that He blesses them, purifies them, has mercy on them, and treats them with kindness.

2. **Mercy from God.**

3. **Guidance.** He guides them toward the right path. He helps them keep their minds clear, their nerves steady, and their will strong. He supports them and takes them by the hand so that they can continue forward and overcome disaster, suffering, temptation, danger, fear, Iblis and his forces, and the self that urges them toward evil.

The same applies to acts of obedience.

When a person remembers God, and remembers the reward He has prepared for obedience—the reward, Paradise, eternal happiness, dignity, honor, and lasting life in this world and the next—that remembrance makes obedience more attractive and helps them bear the difficulty involved.

The same is true when facing sin.

If a person remembers their Lord, remembers that God has forbidden the act before them, and remembers the judgment and punishment that may follow, then fear and reverence awaken within them. That remembrance gives them the patience to hold back.

All of these meanings are brought together in a saying attributed to the Messenger of God:

> “Patience has four branches: longing, fear, detachment, and expectation. Whoever longs for Paradise lets go of desires. Whoever fears the Fire avoids what is forbidden. Whoever is detached from this world takes calamities lightly. And whoever expects death hastens toward good deeds.” [^part11-p009-n1]

Someone who remembers death understands that time is limited. None of us knows how much life remains. So they make use of the time they have and hurry toward what is good.

The third thing that helps us develop patience is **the right understanding of life**.

One of the most important truths to keep before us is that life in this world is limited. It passes. It ends.

If a person keeps that understanding alive in their mind, even the greatest calamities become easier to bear. Everything in this world—what we gain, what we lose, what we acquire, what is taken from us—is temporary.

When this understanding rests on a sound religious view of life, it reduces the weight of calamity and strengthens our ability to endure it.

The final point, also mentioned in the traditions, is that we must learn to control what stands opposite patience: **despairing panic and uncontrolled grief**.

A teaching attributed to Imam Ali makes the point that panic changes nothing in what has already been decreed. The calamity has happened. Whether we lose control or remain patient, the reality itself does not change.

Grief does not bring the dead back to life. It does not restore money we have lost. It does not return occupied land.

And beyond that, there is no spiritual reward in losing control. It brings no benefit in this world and no benefit in the next.

Patience is different.

We have already spoken about its reward in the next life:

> “The patient will be given their reward without measure.” [^part11-p010-n1]

And in this world, patience allows us to continue living and acting. We pray for those who have died and ask God to have mercy on them. We work to recover our land, our resources, our dignity, our security, and everything else we have lost. We may even work to replace what was lost with something better.

## Practical Guidance for the World We Live In

The last thing I want to discuss is what all of this requires from us in practice.

We face many challenges, especially in this difficult age. And those challenges place real duties on us—religious duties and duties of conduct, both personally and collectively.

Every one of us bears responsibility for our families, for the people around us, and for the future and fate of our society.

That responsibility does not belong only to religious scholars or political leaders. Every Muslim carries a share of it.

What makes the matter even more serious is that the responsibilities we face today are, in many ways, harder than those faced by earlier generations. Fulfilling them takes enormous effort.

At the same time, opportunities for wrongdoing are more widespread than ever.

I do not think there has ever been an age in which the means of committing sins, the opportunities to fall into them, and the sheer number of temptations available to people have been as great as they are today.

A person today can be exposed to temptation every minute, even every second.

Believers throughout history have rarely faced an environment like this. Perhaps ours is the kind of age described in the saying of the Prophet that a time will come when holding on to one’s religion will be like holding a burning coal.

Just as it is painful and difficult to hold a burning coal in your hand, the believer in an age like this finds it difficult to hold firmly to their faith.

The reason is simple: wherever a person turns, the opportunity for wrongdoing is there.

It is there at home, while watching television, while reading a newspaper or magazine, and above all through the internet and social media, which today reach into almost every home.

Every possible avenue is open.

This is especially true in a country such as Lebanon, where these matters are not tightly regulated, and where any attempt at regulation is met by people invoking freedom of opinion and public liberties.

In the past, parents who wanted to protect their children from moral corruption could simply choose not to have a television at home. If they did buy one, they could control which channels were available.

That age is over.

The age when you could simply ban a book, a magazine, or a television channel is also over. In the age of communications and the internet, there is no going back to that world.

The danger is now everywhere, and the doors to wrongdoing stand open: through books, pages, words, images, songs, speeches, and much more.

And now all of it can be carried in a pocket.

A boy or girl can carry a phone and, at any moment, open whatever destructive website they want, hear whatever they want, see whatever they want, and speak to whomever they want.

So we can say that never in human history has Iblis had available to him what he has today: so many human and unseen helpers, so many tools of temptation, corruption, and deception, all pushing people toward disobedience to God and obedience to the lower self.

We have never lived through an age quite like this one.

What is true of sin is also true of calamity.

Human beings today are exposed to disaster on a scale previous generations did not know.

In the past, killing a thousand people in war might have required days or even weeks. Today, a single air strike on a populated area can kill a thousand people in minutes.

Modern technology and industry have greatly multiplied both the number and the destructive power of things that can harm human beings.

Disease, microbes, wars, devastating weapons, the media, the ability to stir up internal conflict, economic warfare, and similar forces—all of these can now threaten entire societies.

Add to that the ambitions of powerful states toward weaker ones.

Today an entire country can be placed under siege. Its population can be deprived of food and driven into the worst conditions imaginable.

We are not speaking in abstractions. These are realities. This is what happens in Gaza, in Yemen, and elsewhere.

The issue is no longer simply one person going hungry, or a few people in a society. Entire societies can be starved.

A country can have a strong economy and then suddenly collapse because of what is sometimes called **economic assassination**, carried out by major powers such as the United States, as has happened in Malaysia and elsewhere. Through certain tactics in global financial markets, the value of a national currency can be driven down, the economy can collapse, and disaster can follow.

So we live in a difficult age.

And in this difficult age, with all its heavy burdens, we need patience above all else.

Patience is one of the first things we must call upon if we are to continue our lives, preserve our faith, continue our struggle, remain firm, and shape our destiny in the way God wants for us in this world and the next.

## A Note on Mixed-Gender Socializing

One of the issues that needs greater care and regulation within our own community is the question of social mixing between men and women.

I want to make clear that I am not trying to impose a way of life on anyone. Everyone is free to choose their religion and the way they want to live.

I am speaking about **my own community**, and specifically about people who consider themselves committed to the teachings and guidance of our religion.

In recent years, we have seen mixed-gender socializing become much more common even among religious people. This is something that deserves another look and greater care.

Today, for example, it may seem perfectly normal for two married colleagues from work to meet for lunch, dinner, or an evening together with their spouses. They may sit together for hours, joking, laughing, and talking.

Many people see nothing unusual in this.

But in some cases—and I am not going into the detailed legal rulings here—this kind of setting can lead to things that religion forbids.

The first problem may be the unregulated nature of the gathering itself. Then there may be forbidden looks or attraction. There can also be joking, jealousy, envy, and other feelings that Satan knows how to exploit.

Over time, this can create serious problems between husbands and wives and may sometimes lead to divorce.

If such gatherings have no boundaries, they can even lead, as happens in many other societies, to unlawful relationships—to what people call marital infidelity, God forbid.

We see this in Western societies that are not governed by religious rules. Social mixing, accompanied by joking, laughter, luxury, and entertainment, can develop into illicit relationships. If those relationships are later discovered, the result may be divorce, the collapse of families, and sometimes even murder, especially in our Eastern societies.

And all of it may begin with a gathering that seemed harmless.

As we said earlier, Iblis usually begins with things that appear small and easy.

He often begins in those uncertain spaces close to the line between what is permitted and what is forbidden, especially because people do not always know exactly where that line falls. In matters like these, defining every boundary can itself be difficult.

So anyone who is genuinely concerned for their family and their religion, and who wants to live by God’s command:

> “Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones.” [^part11-p017-n1]

should stay away even from doubtful situations and exercise caution rather than place themselves in circumstances that may later draw them into what is clearly forbidden.

I do not want to go into every detail. We have seen, and continue to see every day, what happens in less conservative societies, including Western ones. If we trace many of these problems back to the beginning, the starting point may have been an inappropriate social setting or a forbidden look.

So we need to address the basic things early rather than wait until we face something much worse.

In our time, with everything broadcast through satellite television and everything circulating online, a husband or wife may gradually become dissatisfied with their spouse. They may stop noticing what is good or attractive in the person beside them because they are constantly comparing them with the images they see every day through these media.

They may then feel unwilling to confine their emotional life to their spouse and begin looking through mixed social settings for contact with other people.

God alone knows where that can lead.

And the risks are not confined to married couples.

We encounter many other situations in universities, schools, workplaces, and homes.

In an environment like the one I have described, we need great care if we want to preserve modesty and the safeguards that protect people.

Suppose, for example, that a religious father has a daughter of sixteen or seventeen and hires a male tutor in his twenties to give her private lessons. They sit together alone for an hour or two.

We mentioned earlier the tradition in which Iblis—who is said to have spoken with Noah, Moses, and other great prophets—was asked when he has the greatest hold over a person.

He answered: when a man is alone with a woman who is not lawful for him.

The tradition continues with Iblis saying:

> “Then I am his companion myself, without sending any of my followers.”

In other words, he considers such a situation such a valuable opportunity that he does not leave it to one of his soldiers.

So this is not something to treat casually.

Even if the tutor is religious and committed, and even if the girl is religious, modest, and honorable, the situation itself may create temptation or suspicion.

According to the traditions, even great worshippers and ascetics have been tested in this way.

So why put ourselves, our young men, our daughters, our wives, and our families in situations like this?

Why expose them unnecessarily?

Why open a door we may later struggle to close?

There are forms of corruption that must be fought once they appear. But sometimes the wiser course is to close the door before corruption enters at all, so that we never reach the stage of having to fight it.

Mixed-gender socializing is one of the areas I wanted to draw attention to because of the damage it can cause when there are no boundaries.

# Conclusion

Whatever the circumstances, we need patience to face the challenges of life.

And if we wanted to reduce the whole lesson of Karbala to one of its greatest themes, patience would be among the first.

The moment Imam Husayn stood before the governor of Medina, described Yazid’s character, and declared:

> “A man like me does not pledge allegiance to a man like him.”

With that, the confrontation began.

And from that moment, the lessons of patience began as well.

There was patience in taking a stand, and patience in accepting the consequences of that stand.

The first consequence in Medina was that Husayn had to gather his family, supporters, companions, and possessions, leave his home and homeland, and separate himself from the rest of his family.

Then came the danger of leaving openly, knowing that he might face a military or security confrontation.

He entered Mecca openly. He stayed there in public. He met people openly.

Yazid eventually became so frustrated by his presence that he sent men to kill him in Mecca, even if they found him clinging to the curtains of the Kaaba.

Exile, migration, leaving one’s homeland, the hardship of travel, and the dangers of the road—all of this required patience from the men, women, and children.

Then came the journey from Mecca toward Kufa, and finally Karbala.

Everything that happened at Karbala—the heroism, the trials, the bloody confrontations, the painful and tragic events—was filled with patience.

And after Karbala came the journey to Kufa, from Kufa to Syria, and from Syria back to Medina.

Every stage was a scene of patience, firmness, endurance, and defiance.

When preachers recount the events of Ashura in deeply mournful terms, there is no doubt that the story is profoundly sad.

But the needs of the mourning gathering and the style of public preaching sometimes lead speakers to exaggerate the human weakness of those involved in order to move the emotions of the audience.

If we want to present the events accurately, however, we have to make something else equally clear.

The story of Husayn certainly contains grief. But it also carries a call, a message, and a defining spirit.

And that spirit is **patience, steadfastness, inner strength, and defiance**.

This is why we must reject portrayals that diminish Lady Zaynab after the martyrdom of Husayn.

I once heard that a preacher had claimed—God forbid—that Zaynab uncovered her head before the people and removed her veil.

Such a claim cannot be accepted about this towering figure of patience and chastity.

Is this really how we want to present Zaynab to the world?

Zaynab, whose patience has no equal?

A woman who behaved in that way would not be Zaynab, daughter of Ali ibn Abi Talib. She would not be Zaynab, daughter of Fatima al-Zahra. She would not be Zaynab, granddaughter of the Messenger of God.

Unfortunately, there are cases in which the Prophet’s family, Imam Husayn, the Prophet himself, Zaynab, and the daughters of Husayn are presented in ways that are impossible to accept.

It is shameful, and it is painful.

**Karbala is patience made beautiful.**  
**Karbala is patience with dignity.**  
**Karbala is the patience of those who overcome.**  
**The patience of those who stand tall.**  
**The patience of the strong.**  
**The patience of believers who preserved this Islam for us, and preserved this community for us.**
